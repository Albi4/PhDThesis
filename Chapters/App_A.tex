\chapter{Magnetic Field Control}
\label{app:MagneticField}

The stability and rapid control of the magnetic field have been crucial for the characterization of the interaction properties of the Dy-K mixture. In this appendix, we describe key improvements made to the magnetic field control during my Ph.D.~work. As for many other parts of this thesis, this was not the work of a single person; I therefore acknowledge Elisa Soave and Zhu-Xiong Ye for the contributions made jointly to the compensation of eddy currents, project started by Cornelis Ravensbergen, and Yaakov Yudkin for the preparation work done regarding the \ac{FPGA} control, which I then implemented in the experiment.


Rapid changes of the magnetic field induce eddy currents in the conductive structures surrounding the magnetic-field coils. These currents circulate along closed paths determined by the geometry and conductivity of the apparatus and, according to Lenz's law, generate a magnetic field opposed to the applied variation. As a consequence, the magnetic field experienced by the atoms does not instantaneously follow the programmed setpoint, but instead exhibits delays and possible distortions following fast ramps. Additionally the power supplies used to generate the magnetic fields and the coil themselves have inherit bandwidth which can limit the response speed. This effect is particularly relevant when working close to Feshbach resonances, where small deviations of the magnetic field can lead to sizable changes of the interaction strength, especially in the case of narrow resonances like the ones encountered in the Dy-K experiment.

Several conductive elements can contribute to eddy currents in an experiment, including the metallic vacuum chamber, optical breadboards, and nearby mounting structures. In our experiment, a first passive mitigation strategy, implemented before the work described in this thesis, consisted of cutting one of the optical breadboards, in order to break a large conductive loop in the vicinity of the coils. This modification reduced the settling time by several tens of milliseconds, but residual eddy-current effects, associated with the remaining metallic structures, in particular with the vacuum chamber, still limited the speed and accuracy of magnetic-field ramps.

To compensate for these residual effects, we characterize the dynamical response of the magnetic field at the position of the atoms. The response is modeled phenomenologically as a linear time-invariant system, where the input $x(t)$ is a programmed magnetic-field waveform, or equivalently, the current command sent to the power supply, and the output $y(t)$ is the magnetic field experienced by the atoms. In this approximation, the two are related by the impulse response $h(t)$ of the apparatus,
\begin{equation}
	y(t) = (h*x)(t),
		\label{eq:convolution}
\end{equation}
where $*$ denotes convolution. In the Laplace domain this relation becomes
\begin{equation}
	Y(s)=H(s)X(s).
		\label{eq:laplacedomain}
\end{equation}
The transfer function can therefore be inferred from the measured response to a known input as
\begin{equation}
	H(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}.
	\label{eq:transferFunc}
\end{equation}

In our experiment, we employ two separate sets of coils, as already mentioned in Sec.~\ref{App:MagneticFieldControl}, to produce and control the magnetic field. The main offset coils and the faster ``jump'' coils have different inductances, power supplies, geometries, and current ranges, and therefore different effective transfer functions. In the experimental sequence, the two coil systems are used for different purposes. The main offset coils generate the background magnetic field required to approach the region of interest, while the smaller jump coils are used to perform precise and fast tuning of the magnetic field around this offset value. 

The response function of both coil systems is modeled according to the phenomenological model presented here, and in both cases the response is parametrized as a sum of exponential components
\begin{equation}
	B(t)=B_{\mathrm{target}}(t)+\sum_i A_i e^{-(t-t_i)/\tau_i}+\sum_j A_j e^{-(t-t_j)/\tau_j},
\end{equation}
where the indices $i$ and $j$ indicate sums over terms associated with the offset coils and the jump coils, respectively, $t_i$ and  $t_j$ indicate the different triggering times for the different coils, and $B_{\mathrm{target}}(t)$ is the desired magnetic field at any time. During the experimental sequence, the offset coils are used before the jump coils, and we typically wait for approximately \SI{100}{ms} before driving the jump coils, to allow the slow transients associated with the offset coils to decay. We therefore treat the two coil systems as independent and characterize them separately, which is justified by the large separation in their characteristic timescales. In the following, we present the characterization of the jump coils as a representative example. The same procedure was applied to the offset coils, although with different resulting parameters.

To calibrate and test magnetic fields in our experiment we typically drive \ac{RF} transitions between Zeeman states in the lowest hyperfine manifold of potassium, in particular, between the stretched ground state $\ket{9/2, -9/2}$ and the first excited  Zeeman sublevel $\ket{9/2, -7/2}$ within the same hyperfine manifold. After applying the magnetic-field ramp under investigation, the atoms are held for a variable time before the \ac{RF} pulse is applied. By scanning the \ac{RF} frequency and determining the resonance center, the magnetic field at the position of the atoms is extracted using the Breit-Rabi formula. This procedure probes the field experienced by the atomic cloud directly.

We extract the transfer function by measuring the response to a fast linear magnetic-field ramp. In the example presented here, the measured response is well reproduced by a two-timescale model, and the transfer function is parametrized as
\begin{equation}
	H(s)=
	\frac{A_1\gamma_1/(s+\gamma_1)+A_2\gamma_2/(s+\gamma_2)}{A_1+A_2},
	\label{eq:eddyTransferFunction}
\end{equation} 
where $A_i$ are the relative amplitudes of the eddy-current contributions and $\gamma_i=1/\tau_i$ are their decay rates. The normalization ensures $H(0)=1$, so that the transfer function does not change the final magnetic-field value, but only describes the finite settling dynamics.

The compensation of the eddy currents for the jump coils is summarized in Fig.~\ref{fig:TFJump_examp}. The blue data points show the magnetic-field response of the experimental setup to a linear magnetic-field ramp of duration $T=\SI{0.4}{ms}$ and amplitude $\Delta B=\SI{175}{mG}$, followed by a constant hold field. In the Laplace domain, the predicted uncompensated response, $B_{\mathrm{actual}}$, is then obtained by applying the measured transfer function,
\begin{equation}
	B_{\mathrm{actual}}(s)=H(s)B_{\mathrm{target}}(s).
	\label{eq:predictedRamp}
\end{equation}
For the data shown here, the resulting parameters of $H(s)$ according to  Eq.~\eqref{eq:eddyTransferFunction}, are
\begin{equation}
	A_1={0.1} \quad \gamma_1=\SI{3.49}{ms^{-1}}\quad
	\text{and}\quad
	A_2={0.025} \quad \gamma_2=\SI{0.5}{ms^{-1}},
	\label{eq:EddySuppParam}
\end{equation}
corresponding to characteristic decay times $\tau_1=\SI{0.29}{ms}$ and $\tau_2=\SI{2.0}{ms}$\,\footnote{It is worth noting here that in the case of the offset coils and for other cases of use of the jump coils, like the one reported in Chapter~\ref{Chpt:DipoleandHydro}, an additional slower component is required, reflecting the larger currents, magnetic-field amplitudes and longer time scales involved in the ramp procedure.}.  
This response is shown as the red solid line in Fig.~\ref{fig:TFJump_examp}, which reproduces well the magnetic-field response measured on the atoms.

The feed-forward waveform is generated by applying the inverse of the effective transfer function to the desired magnetic-field trajectory. If $B_{\mathrm{target}}(s)$ is the Laplace transform of the desired field ramp and $H(s)$ is the measured response function of the coil system, the programmed waveform is calculated as
\begin{equation}
	B_{\mathrm{cmd}}(s)=\frac{B_{\mathrm{target}}(s)}{H(s)}.
\end{equation}
In the ideal linear-response limit, the magnetic field at the atoms should be the desired one. In practice, this procedure provides an initial feed-forward correction, which is then refined empirically using RF spectroscopy on the atoms, for example to avoid an overshoot of the field when working near the pole of a narrow Feshbach resonance. 	

The red data points in Fig.~\ref{fig:TFJump_examp} show the result of the compensation procedure with feed-forward correction. The measured field reaches the target value already within the earliest measured hold times, with residual deviations of only a few \unit{mG}. The compensation therefore substantially improves the reproducibility and speed of magnetic-field ramps, which is essential for the characterization of narrow features in the Dy-K interaction spectrum.

As an additional improvement on the magnetic field control, the target trajectory for the final implementation in the experiment is chosen to be a smooth minimum-jerk ramp \cite{sharkawy2021mjt}
\begin{equation}
 B(t)=B_0+(B_{f}-B_0)\left(10	\left(\frac{t}{T}	\right)^3-15\left(\frac{t}{T}\right)^4+6\left(\frac{t}{T}\right)^5\right),
	\label{eq:MinJerkFun}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the ramp duration, $B_0$ is the initial magnetic field (or equivalently the initial current flowing in the coils) and $B_{f}$ is the desired final set point. We prefer this profile over a strictly linear ramp to suppress high-frequency components that could be amplified by the inverse filter, and more generally to have smoother ramps, including cases where feed-forward compensation is not required.

In order to implement the suppression procedure detailed in this appendix, programmable current controls are necessary in order to perform the calculated feed-forward ramp. In our case, the numeric infrastructure is implemented in MATLAB which calculates the waveforms required for the experiment and transmits them to the current controllers. For the main offset coils, we use a programmable power supply\,\footnote{Els instruments Fast-PS-$1$K$5$ $100$-$15$}, capable of providing large currents with fast ramps and featuring a digital \ac{PID} interface, which allows for fine tuning of the \ac{PID} control parameters. For the precise, smaller ramps of the jump coils, we instead use a smaller lab 	power supply controlled with a homemade digital \ac{PID} based on a \ac{FPGA} infrastructure developed in Ref.~\cite{Thomas2020adf}. This feedback control not only allows us to program the current waveforms to compensate for eddy currents, but also enables full calibration of the \ac{PID} parameters for different ramp speeds and amplitudes.

The compensation procedure we presented in this appendix has been quite successful in our experiment and most of the results obtained were indeed enabled by this method, both for the exploration of large portions of the interaction spectrum and the fine-tuning of the scattering length and molecular state near the $7$-G resonance. However, this method has some shortcomings. In particular, the digital control of the magnetic field, both for the offset coils and the jump coils, in several situations resulted in some bottlenecking of the experimental cycle. The finite memory available on the \ac{FPGA} chip, specifically, allows magnetic field control only up to \SI{5}{s}, and for longer hold times the communication with the power supply or the \ac{FPGA}-based control, could occasionally require more time than a standard experimental cycle. Some of these shortcomings, in particular regarding the communication times, have been overcome, thanks to improvements to the native software of the digitally controlled power supply, and to improvements made in the last year to our MATLAB code. 

Despite these limitations, the infrastructure developed here enabled the key results of this thesis and laid the groundwork for further improvements. A second generation experiment could overcome some of these shortcomings by integrating the control architecture more tightly with the experimental cycle and with the software infrastructure. Additionally, a second-generation experiment, as envisioned in Chapter~\ref{Chpt:Summary}, could reduce the magnitude of the eddy currents by using a glass vacuum chamber and by choosing to work near a specific Feshbach resonance, and designing the coils accordingly for the required magnetic field range.
