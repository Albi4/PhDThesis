\section{Eddy Current compensation}
\label{Sec:EddyCurrents}

Rapid changes of the magnetic field induce eddy currents in the conductive structures surrounding the magnetic-field coils. These currents circulate along closed paths determined by the geometry and conductivity of the apparatus and, according to Lenz's law, generate a magnetic field opposed to the applied variation. As a consequence, the magnetic field experienced by the atoms does not instantaneously follow the programmed current waveform, but instead exhibits delays and possible distortions after fast ramps. This effect is particularly relevant when working close to Feshbach resonances, where small deviations of the magnetic field can lead to sizable changes of the interaction strength, even more so for the case of narrow resonances, like the one studied in the Dy-K experiment.

Several conductive elements can contribute to eddy currents in an experiment, including the metallic vacuum chamber, optical breadboards, and nearby mounting structures. In our experiment, a first passive mitigation strategy, implemented before the work described in this thesis, consisted in cutting one of the optical breadboards, placed in the proximity of the vacuum chamber, in order to interrupt large conductive loops close to the coils. This modification significantly reduced the amplitude of the induced currents, but residual eddy-current effects, associated with the remaining metallic structures, still limited the speed and accuracy of magnetic-field ramps.

To compensate for these residual effects, we characterized the dynamical response of the magnetic field at the position of the atoms. The response was modeled phenomenologically as a linear time-invariant system, where the input $x(t)$ is a programmed magnetic-field waveform, or equivalently the current command sent to the power supply, and the output $y(t)$ is the magnetic field experienced by the atoms. In this approximation, the two are related by the impulse response $h(t)$ of the apparatus,

\begin{equation}
	y(t) = (h*x)(t),
		\label{eq:convolution}
\end{equation}
where $*$ denotes convolution. In the Laplace domain this relation becomes
\begin{equation}
	Y(s)=H(s)X(s).
		\label{eq:lamplacedomain}
\end{equation}
 The transfer function can therefore be inferred from the measured response to a known input as
\begin{equation}
	H(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}.
	\label{eq:transferFunc}
\end{equation}

In practice, in our experiment we employ two separated sets of coils, as already mentioned in Sec.~\ref{App:MagneticFieldControl}, to produce and control the magnetic field. The main offset coils and the faster ``jump'' coils have different inductances, power supplies, geometries, and current ranges, and therefore different effective transfer functions. The response function extracted in this way should thus be understood as a phenomenological model of a specific coil--power-supply system, including its coupling to the surrounding conductive structures, rather than as a universal response of the apparatus.

\section{A digital PID for fast magnetic field control}
\label{Sec:FPGA}


Final step was to use what learned from having a fast power supply with a digital \ac{PID} in combination with a smaller set of coils in order to perform quick and and precise ramps of few tens to hundreds of \unit{mG} to precisely control interactions. 

The large power supply used to control the main coils is limited in resolution to perform small magnetic field ramps necessary to handle narrow magnetic Feshbach resonance. Our solution was to use the large coils to provide an off-set field and use a smaller power supply controlled with an home-made digital \ac{PID} based on a \ac{FPGA}, taking from an idea reported in Ref.~\cite{Thomas2020adf}, to do smaller magnetic field ramps.
