%\section{Eddy Current compensation}
%\label{Sec:EddyCurrents}

Rapid changes of the magnetic field induce eddy currents in the conductive structures surrounding the magnetic-field coils. These currents circulate along closed paths determined by the geometry and conductivity of the apparatus and, according to Lenz's law, generate a magnetic field opposed to the applied variation. As a consequence, the magnetic field experienced by the atoms does not instantaneously follow the programmed setpoint, but instead exhibits delays and possible distortions following fast ramps. This effect is particularly relevant when working close to Feshbach resonances, where small deviations of the magnetic field can lead to sizable changes of the interaction strength, especially in the case for narrow resonances like the ones encountered in the Dy-K experiment.

Several conductive elements can contribute to eddy currents in an experiment, including the metallic vacuum chamber, optical breadboards, and nearby mounting structures. In our experiment, a first passive mitigation strategy, implemented before the work described in this thesis, consisted of cutting one of the optical breadboards, in order to break a large conductive loop in the vicinity of the coils. This modification reduced the settling time by several tens of milliseconds, but residual eddy-current effects, associated with the remaining metallic structures, still limited the speed and accuracy of magnetic-field ramps.

To compensate for these residual effects, we characterized the dynamical response of the magnetic field at the position of the atoms. The response was modeled phenomenologically as a linear time-invariant system, where the input $x(t)$ is a programmed magnetic-field waveform, or equivalently, the current command sent to the power supply, and the output $y(t)$ is the magnetic field experienced by the atoms. In this approximation, the two are related by the impulse response $h(t)$ of the apparatus,
\begin{equation}
	y(t) = (h*x)(t),
		\label{eq:convolution}
\end{equation}
where $*$ denotes convolution. In the Laplace domain this relation becomes
\begin{equation}
	Y(s)=H(s)X(s).
		\label{eq:laplacedomain}
\end{equation}
The transfer function can therefore be inferred from the measured response to a known input as
\begin{equation}
	H(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}.
	\label{eq:transferFunc}
\end{equation}

In our experiment, we employ two separate sets of coils, as already mentioned in Sec.~\ref{App:MagneticFieldControl}, to produce and control the magnetic field. The main offset coils and the faster ``jump'' coils have different inductances, power supplies, geometries, and current ranges, and therefore different effective transfer functions. In the experimental sequence, the two coil systems are used for different purposes. The main offset coils generate the background magnetic field required to approach the region of interest, while the smaller jump coils are used to perform precise tuning of the magnetic field around this offset value. 

The response function of both coil systems is modeled according to the phenomenological model presented here, and in both cases the response is parametrized as a sum of exponential components
\begin{equation}
	B(t)=B_{f}+\sum_i A_i e^{-t/\tau_i}+\sum_j A_j e^{-t/\tau_j},
\end{equation}
where the indices $i$ and $j$ indicate sums over terms associated with the offset coils and the jump coils, respectively, and $B_f$ is the desired equilibrium field. During the experimental sequence, the offset coils are used before the jump coils, and we typically wait for approximately \SI{100}{ms} before driving the jump coils, to allow the slow transients associated with the offset coils to decay. We therefore treat the two coil systems as independent and characterize them separately, which is justified by the large separation in their characteristic timescales. In the following, we present the characterization of the jump coils as a representative example. The same procedure was applied to the offset coils, although with different resulting parameters.

To calibrate and test magnetic fields in our experiment we typically drive \ac{RF} transitions between Zeeman states in the lowest hyperfine manifold of potassium, in particular, between the stretched ground state $\ket{9/2, -9/2}$ and the first excited  Zeeman sublevel $\ket{9/2, -7/2}$ within the same hyperfine manifold. After applying the magnetic-field ramp under investigation, the atoms are held for a variable time before the \ac{RF} pulse is applied. By scanning the \ac{RF} frequency and determining the resonance center, the magnetic field at the position of the atoms is extracted using the Breit-Rabi formula. This procedure probes the field experienced by the atomic cloud directly.

We extract the transfer function of our system by measuring the response of our experimental setup to a step magnetic field ramp, for the case consider in this example we use a jump with an amplitude of \SI{175}{mG}. The measured response is well reproduced by a two-timescale model, and the transfer function is parametrized as
\begin{equation}
	H(s)=
	\frac{A_1\gamma_1/(s+\gamma_1)+A_2\gamma_2/(s+\gamma_2)}{A_1+A_2},
	\label{eq:eddyTransferFunction}
\end{equation} 
where $A_i$ are the relative amplitudes of the eddy-current contributions and $\gamma_i=1/\tau_i$ are their decay rates. The normalization ensures $H(0)=1$, so that the transfer function does not change the final magnetic-field value, but only describes the finite settling dynamics.
For the data shown here, the fitted parameters are
\begin{equation}
	A_1={0.1} \quad \gamma_1=\SI{3.49}{ms^{-1}}\quad
\text{and}\quad
	A_2={0.025} \quad \gamma_2=\SI{0.5}{ms^{-1}},
	\label{eq:EddySuppParam}
\end{equation}
corresponding to characteristic decay times $\tau_1=\SI{0.29}{ms}$ and $\tau_2=\SI{2.0}{ms}$\, \footnote{It is worth noting here that in the case of the offset coils and for other case of use of the jump coils, like the one reported in Chapter~\ref{Chpt:DipoleandHydro}, an additional slower component is required, reflecting the larger currents, magnetic-field amplitudes  and longer time scales involved.}.

The compensation of the eddy currents for the jump coils is summarized in Fig.~\ref{fig:TFJump_examp}. The blue data points show the magnetic-field response of the experimental setup to a linear magnetic-field ramp of duration $T=\SI{2}{ms}$ and amplitude $\Delta B=\SI{175}{mG}$, followed by a constant hold field. In the Laplace domain, the ideal target ramp is given by
\begin{equation}
	B_{\mathrm{target}}(s)=\frac{\Delta B}{T}\frac{1-e^{-Ts}}{s^2}.
	\label{eq:rampLaplace}
\end{equation}
The predicted uncompensated response is then obtained by applying the measured transfer function,
\begin{equation}
	B_{\mathrm{pred}}(s)=H(s)B_{\mathrm{target}}(s).
	\label{eq:predictedRamp}
\end{equation}
Using the parameters of Eq.~\eqref{eq:EddySuppParam}, this response is shown as the red solid line in Fig.~\ref{fig:TFJump_examp}, which reproduces well the magnetic-field response measured on the atoms.

This model was used as a starting point to generate feed-forward corrected waveforms. Rather than relying on an exact analytical inversion of the transfer function, which would be limited in practice by the finite bandwidth and voltage range of the current supply, the feed-forward waveform was optimized experimentally. The optimization was performed by iteratively modifying the programmed ramp and measuring the resulting magnetic field at the position of the atoms.



Figure~\ref{fig:eddyResponseFit} shows the measured magnetic-field response after a rapid change of the programmed field, together with the two-timescale model of Eq.~\eqref{eq:eddyTransferFunction}. The model captures the main relaxation dynamics of the magnetic field and provides the initial estimate used for the feed-forward correction.

The effect of the optimized feed-forward waveform is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:eddyFeedForward}. Without compensation, the magnetic field initially undershoots the target value and relaxes only over a timescale of several milliseconds. With feed-forward compensation, the measured field reaches the target value already within the earliest measured hold times, with residual deviations of only a few \unit{mG}. The compensation therefore substantially improves the reproducibility and speed of magnetic-field ramps, which is essential for the characterization of narrow features in the Dy-K interaction spectrum.

%\section{A digital PID for fast magnetic field control}
%\label{Sec:FPGA}


Final step was to use what learned from having a fast power supply with a digital \ac{PID} in combination with a smaller set of coils in order to perform quick and and precise ramps of few tens to hundreds of \unit{mG} to precisely control interactions. 

The large power supply used to control the main coils is limited in resolution to perform small magnetic field ramps necessary to handle narrow magnetic Feshbach resonance. Our solution was to use the large coils to provide an off-set field and use a smaller power supply controlled with an home-made digital \ac{PID} based on a \ac{FPGA}, taking from an idea reported in Ref.~\cite{Thomas2020adf}, to do smaller magnetic field ramps.
