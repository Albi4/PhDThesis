In particular, across the two years of $1925$ and $1926$, multiple essential papers contributed to shape the foundations of modern quantum mechanics, stemming, but at the same time being radically different, from the ''old'' quantum theory introduced at the beginning of the century, and tracing a net separation  from the deterministic approach of classical mechanics.

The year $1925$ was a particularly important year for the field of quantum physics. Through the contributions of Heisenberg, Born, Schr\"odinger and many others, decisive steps forward were made towards a deeper theoretical footing of quantum physics. In particular, Heisenberg's ``Umdeutung'' paper \albi{ref.} drew a sharp distinction from the deterministic approach of classical mechanics. 

In the same year, Wolfgang Pauli attempted to provide a theoretical basis for   Bohr's empirical closed-shell atomic model, and at the same time to understand the Zeeman effect. He postulated the \textit{Pauli exclusion principle} \albi{ref.}, which states that only a single electron in an atomic orbital can occupy a specific state, that is, no two electrons can share the same set of quantum numbers.

A year after Pauli, exactly a hundred years ago, Fermi \albi{cit}  and Dirac \albi{cit} independently extended Pauli's results to the case of a non-interacting gas consisting of indistinguishable particles, obeying the exclusion principle. These results laid the groundwork for what is now known as Fermi-Dirac statistics, that describes the thermodynamic behavior, at the quantum level, of particles with half-integer spin, known as fermions.

This framework is complementary to the one proposed, around the same time, first by Bose \albi{cit.} and later extended by Einstein \albi{cit.}. They considered the situation in which particles are fully indistinguishable and are allowed to be all in the same quantum state. In this case particles with integer spin, called bosons, follow the Bose-Einstein statistics.

The deep connection between the spin of a particle and the statistics it obeys  was formulated by Pauli in the spin-statistic theorem. The theorem implies that the quantum theory is not only a probabilistic theory, but one with inherently statistical mechanical character. While these results are valid for all physical objects for which a quantum description is required, in this work we will focus on atoms and the weakly bound diatomic molecules they can form.


Atoms are one of the most suited systems to study the interplay between quantum statistics and many-body quantum mechanics \albi{explain more, some reviews ref here}. To experimentally observe these kinds of phenomena, ultra-low temperatures, of the order of hundreds of nanokelvin and lower, are needed. At these temperatures quantum mechanical effects become predominant and give rise to macroscopic effects. Owing to the wave-particle duality of matter, it is possible to associate to an atom its thermal de Broglie wavelength, expressed as
\begin{equation}
	\lambda_{\textrm th}=\frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}}\,,
\end{equation}
with $h$ the Planck constant, $k_B$ the Boltzmann constant, $m$ the mass of atoms and $T$ the temperature. When $\lambda_{\textrm th}$ is of the order of the interparticle distance, i.e. $\lambda_{\textrm th}\sim n^{-1/3}$, with 
$n$ the number density of the gas, the quantum statistical nature of the atoms plays a fundamental role.

The development of laser cooling techniques in the 80s and 90s allowed to cool atomic samples to this temperatures regime and starting from $1995$ 
some of the most spectacular results in quantum anybody physics were obtained, starting with the observation of \ac{BEC} in a dilute ultracold quantum gas of sodium and rubidium in 1995 \albi{add citations} and the production of a degenerate Fermi gas of potassium first and lithium short after.  \albi{add citations}

An experiment, exemplifying the role of quantum statistics in the ultra-low temperature regime, was performed, at Rice university, with a mixture of two isotopes of lithium, one fermionic, $^6$Li, and one bosonic, $^7$Li. The mixture was cooled to the ultracold regime and kept at thermal equilibrium. Temperature was then lowered, through evaporative cooling, and the difference in statistics becomes clear. while the cloud with the bosonic isotopes shrinks and forms a \ac{BEC}, the cloud  with the fermionic atoms, despite getting colder keeps its size, as the Fermi pressure due to the Pauli exclusion principle
