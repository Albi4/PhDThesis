%Around a century ago, the best minds physics could muster were focusing their efforts to understand the world using the recently introduced concepts of quantum mechanics. In particular, across the two years of $1925$ and $1926$, multiple essential papers contributed to shape the foundations of modern quantum mechanics, stemming, but at the same time being radically different, from the ''old'' quantum theory introduced at the beginning of the century, and tracing a net separation  from the deterministic approach of classical mechanics.

$1925$ was a particularly important year for the field of quantum physics. Thanks to the effort of many scientists, a decisive step forward was made towards a more fundamental interpretation to quantum physics, in particular with Heisenberg's ``Umdeutung'' paper \albi{ref.}, that traced a net separation from the deterministic approach of classical mechanics. 

In the same year, Wolfgang Pauli, trying to theoretically motivate the empirical closed-shell atomic model of Bohr, and at the same time to understand the Zeeman effect, theorized the \textit{exclusion principle} \albi{ref.}, which nowadays we name after him. In his paper Pauli postulated a criterion for which only a single electron, in an atomic orbital, can occupy a determined state, that is, only  a single electron can have a unique set of quantum numbers.

A year after Pauli, exactly hundred years ago, Fermi \albi{cit}  and Dirac \albi{cit}, independently, extended Pauli results to the case of a non interacting gas consisting of indistinguishable particles, obeying to the exclusion principle. This results would lay the foundation of what today we call the Fermi-Dirac statistics, that describes the thermodynamic behavior of particles with half-integer spin, called fermions.

This set of results is complementary to the case proposed, around the same time, first by Bose \albi{cit.} and followed up by Einstein \albi{cit.}. They considerate the situation in which particles are fully indistinguishable and are allowed to be all in the same quantum state. In this case the particles, with the integer spin number, follow the Bose-Einstein statistics and are called Bosons.

The deep connection between the spin of a particle and  the statistics it obeys,  was formulated by Pauli, in the famous spin-statistic theorem.   the theorem implies that the quantum theory is not only a probabilistic theory, but also it has inherently  a statistical mechanical implications.
