\section{Basics of Fermi gases}
\label{sec:FermiGases}

An ensemble of identical fermions is a system of perfectly non-interacting particles, with the requirement that no more than a single atom can occupy a given energy state.Throughout this thesis, we consider gases confined in a three-dimensional harmonic trap. In the limit of $T\to0$, a non-interacting fermionic gas will occupy the available states $\varepsilon_i$ in the harmonic trap, according to the Fermi-Dirac distribution
\begin{equation}	
	f(\varepsilon_i)=\frac{1}{\exp{(\varepsilon_i-\mu)/k_BT}+1},
	\label{eq:FermiDiracDist}
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is chemical potential, and its normalization is imposed by the condition $	N=\int d\varepsilon\frac{\varepsilon^2}{2(\hbar\omega)^3}f(\varepsilon)$.

This distribution is in stark contrast with the one for a Bose gas, where the $+1$ in the denominator is replaced by $-1$. The difference in sign indicates the different occupancy probability between fermions and bosons and allows, in the limit $T\to0$, for bosons to accumulate in the ground state, while for fermions it enforces single occupancy of each energy state.

The result is that, even at $T=0$, a fermionic system will have a finite amount of energy, a direct reflection of the Pauli exclusion principle. The energy associated with the resulting distribution is the \textit{Fermi Energy} 
\begin{equation}
	E_F=\hbar\bar{\omega}(6N)^{1/3},
	\label{eq:FermiEnergy}
\end{equation}
where  $\bar{\omega}=(\omega_x\omega_y\omega_z)^{1/3}$ is the geometric mean of the trap frequencies. From the Fermi energy, we can define the Fermi momentum	
\begin{equation}
	k_F=\frac{\sqrt{2mE_F}}{\hbar}.
	\label{eq:FermiMomentum}
\end{equation}

Similarly, we can also define the Fermi temperature 
\begin{equation}
	T_F=E_F/{k_B}.
	\label{eq:FermiTemperature}
\end{equation}
Usually in an ultracold gas experiment the temperature is the most readily measurable quantity. The degree of degeneracy of a Fermi gas is therefore commonly quantified by the ratio $T/T_F$, if the measured temperature of the gas is below the Fermi temperature, $T/T_F<1$, the gas is said to be degenerate. This is analogous to the bosonic case, where degeneracy is signaled by $T<T_c$, however, unlike bosons, fermions do not undergo a phase transition and the crossover to degeneracy is smooth.

The density distribution of a non-interacting degenerate Fermi gas deviates from the Gaussian profile characteristic of a thermal gas. In the bosonic case, the difference between the degenerate and thermal distributions is dramatic, reflecting the sharp phase transition to a \ac{BEC}. For the fermionic case, instead, the deviation is more subtle. In practice, this deviation from a Gaussian profile serves as a direct experimental measure of the degree of degeneracy. In practice, to obtain a measure of degeneracy, we routinely fit the two-dimensional column density profiles obtained from absorption imaging with a polylogarithmic function \cite{Ketterle2008mpa}
\begin{equation}
	n(x,y) = -A\,\mathrm{Li}_2\left(-z \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2\sigma_x^2} - \frac{y^2}{2\sigma_y^2}\right)\right),
	\label{eq:PolyLog}
\end{equation} 
where $Li_2$ is the polylogarithm of order $2$, A is an amplitude, $z=e^{\mu/k_BT}$ is the fugacity, and $\sigma_{x,y}$ are the thermal widths. The fugacity $z$ encodes the degeneracy: for $z\ll 1$ the distribution reduces to a Gaussian (classical limit), while for $z\gg1$ the distribution approaches the zero-temperature Thomas-Fermi profile.


It is worth noting here that these definitions and quantities are for a single component Fermi gas. \section{Feshbach resonances in ultracold gases}
\label{sec:FeshbachResIntro}


Magnetically tunable Feshbach resonances are one of the central tools of ultracold-atom experiments. As discussed in the previous chapters, they allow the interaction strength between two atoms to be controlled by changing an external magnetic field. At the two-body level, this tunability originates from the coupling between an open scattering channel and a molecular bound state in a closed channel. When the energy of this bound state is tuned close to the scattering threshold, the scattering length becomes resonantly enhanced and can be varied over a large range. On one side of the resonance, the two atoms support a weakly bound molecular state, commonly referred to as a Feshbach molecule.
