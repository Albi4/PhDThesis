While much of the recently added complexity in ultracold experiments is deliberately engineered, mostly in the form of a \ac{QGM} for single-atom control and imaging, the complexity of the Dy-K setup is largely intrinsic, inherited from the atomic species and the physics under investigation. Ours is the first experiment to investigate a quantum mixture of an alkali with a lanthanide. In a standard ultracold experiment working with an alkali atom, laser cooling typically requires only one or two lasers, supplemented by one or two high-power laser beams for trapping and evaporative cooling. Adding a second species to such a setup requires additional resonant laser sources for the different transitions of each atomic species. Moreover, lanthanide atoms like dysprosium, erbium, europium \albi{cite} present a richer electronic structure, which on one hand can be used to produce laser-cooled sub-\unit{\micro\K} atomic samples, but on the other hand increases the experimental complexity, as is the case in our setup. The Dy-K experiment faced the dual challenge of lacking any theoretical prediction of the interaction properties and of characterizing an entirely new type of quantum mixture. With the ultimate goal of exploring unconventional superfluidity in a mass-imbalanced Fermi mixture, many obstacles had to be overcome, the most significant of which are outlined below.
 
When I joined the team at the end of $2021$, the experiment was already fully operational. Several important results had already been obtained, in particular regarding the preparation of the doubly-degenerate mixture \cite{Ravensbergen2018poa}, the precise measurement of dysprosium polarizability \cite{Ravensbergen2018ado, Kreyer2021mot}, the observation of the first interspecies Feshbach resonances \cite{Ravensbergen2020rif}, and the characterization of Dy-Dy intraspecies interactions \cite{Soave2022lff}. In  Sec.~\ref{Sec:ExpSeq},  we briefly describe the procedure we employ to produce the doubly-degenerate Fermi mixture, while in Sec.~\ref{Sec:DyKFeshbachOld}, we describe the results already obtained concerning the investigation of interaction in the mixture \cite{Ravensbergen2020rif} and in Dy alone \cite{Soave2022lff}, which set the stage for the studies presented in the following chapters.
 
 \section{Experimental sequence at a glance}
 \label{Sec:ExpSeq}
 In this section we give an overview of the sequence routinely used in our experiment to produce a doubly-degenerate mixture of dysprosium and potassium. This procedure represents the cumulative effort of past and current members of the group \cite{Ravensbergen2018poa,Ravensbergen2020PhD, Tzanova2020PhD, Kreyer2017Master, Soave2022PhD}, and it is presented here in its most current form.
 
In our experiment we use one of the two fermionic isotopes of dysprosium, $^{161}$Dy, combined with the only fermionic isotope of potassium, $^{40}$K. We employ an \textit{all-optical} approach to cool the mixture to degeneracy. Owing to the different magnetic moments of the two atomic species, the magnetic field gradients applied during the \ac{MOT} differ significantly between the two species, which necessitates sequential loading of the two species. Since the magnetic gradient used for potassium is about $5$ times stronger than the one needed for dysprosium, it is convenient to first prepare the K \ac{MOT}, followed by the Dy \ac{MOT}, in order to avoid causing losses in the Dy cloud. 

Potassium atomic source is a $2$D \ac{MOT} prepared in a separate glass cell, loaded from an enriched sample of $^{40}$K\,\footnote{The natural abundance of $^{40}$K is about $0.01\%$, so an enriched sample is necessary to work with the fermionic isotope.}. A pus-beam sub-sequentially pushes the atomic cloud in the main chamber, where it is captured by the $3$D \ac{MOT}. Both $3$D and $2$D \ac{MOT}s and the push-beam operate on the potassium D$2$ line at \SI{767}{nm} (linewidth $\Gamma_{D2}/2\pi\sim\SI{6}{MHz}$), and all the laser beams are generated by the same source\,\footnote{Toptica TA Pro}. After the \ac{MOT}, potassium is further cooled via a gray molasses stage on the D$1$ line (wavelength \SI{770}{nm} with linewidth $\Gamma_{D1}/2\pi\sim\SI{6}{MHz}$)\,\footnote{The $770$-nm light is produced from frequency doubling a NKT fiber laser (Koheras ADJUSTIK E$15$).}, achieving temperatures of around \SI{30}{\micro K}, with an almost ($\sim80\%$) spin polarized sample in the lowest hyperfine state $\ket{{F}, {m_F}}=\ket{9/2,-9/2}$, with the residual population in the second lowest state  $\ket{9/2,-7/2}$. Simultaneously, to maximize the number of captured atoms, the K cloud is transferred into a large-volume crossed \ac{ODT}, which operates  in the near infrared at a wavelength of \SI{1064}{nm}. In Fig.~\ref{fig:ODTscheme}, we summarize the complex \ac{ODT} scheme employed in our experiment. The reservoir trap is composed by the beams labeled ``IPG'' and ``Shallow'' for historical reasons, and are produced by the same laser source\,\footnote{Azurlight ALS-IR-$1064$-$50$-I-CC-SF}.

Once potassium is trapped in the reservoir \ac{ODT}, the preparation of the Dy cloud can start. The dysprosium atomic source is a high-temperature effusion oven, operating at around $1000\,^\circ$C. The atomic beam is then decelerated by a Zeeman slower that operates on the broad transition at \SI{421}{nm} ($\Gamma_{421}/2\pi\sim\SI{32}{MHz}$)\,\footnote{Toptica TA-SHG PRO}. The slowed atomic beam is captured in a $3$D \ac{MOT} generated at the narrow intercombination transition at \SI{626}nm (linewidth $\Gamma_{626}/2\pi\sim\SI{135}{kHz}$)\,\footnote{The $626$-nm light is produce from a frequency summation of two infrared fiber NKT lasers, at \SI{1050}{nm} (Koheras BoostiK Y$10$) and \SI{1550}{nm} (Koheras BoostiK E$15$).}. Thanks to the narrowness of the transition used in the \ac{MOT}, the cold atomic sample obtained is naturally spin-polarized in the lowest hyperfine state \cite{Dreon2017oca}, that is the stretched state $\ket{{F}, {m_F}}=\ket{21/2, -21/2}$. At the end of the compressed \ac{MOT} stage, the temperature of the atomic cloud is approximately \SI{8}{\micro K}. During the compressed stage of the \ac{MOT}, the Dy cloud is transferred in the same reservoir \ac{ODT} as K. 

Before evaporative cooling, Dy undergoes an additional laser cooling stage\,\footnote{This additional stage was implemented in the experiment before I joined the team, but reported for the first time in Ref.~\cite{Ye2022ool}, which is part of this thesis as Chapter \ref{Chpt:Lowfieldpaper}.}, performed near the narrow transition at \SI{741}{nm}, with linewidth $\Gamma_{741}/2\pi\sim\,\SI{1.7}{kHz}$\,\footnote{Moglabs ECDL} \cite{Lu2011soa}. We perform this cooling stage after transferring the atomic clouds into an elongated \ac{ODT}, a combination of the ``IPG'' and ``H-MOPA'' beams, see Fig.~\ref{fig:ODTscheme}. Thanks to this cooling stage, we achieve temperatures of around \SI{1}{\micro K}. More details on the narrow-line cooling sequence are reported in Sec. \ref{sec:appB}. It is worth noting here that, while as reported in Ref.~\cite{Ye2022ool} no effect of the \SI{741}{nm} light is observed on the potassium cloud, the rapid change of the trap, from the reservoir configuration to the elongated one, is detrimental to the potassium cloud, which loses a significant number of atoms. This does not strictly affect the efficiency of evaporative cooling, and has not been a limiting factor in most of the experiments presented in this thesis, but limits the tunability of the potassium atom number. This limitation and potential solutions are discussed in Chapter \ref{Chpt:Summary}. 

At the end of the \SI{741}{nm} cooling stage, the atomic mixture is transferred from the elongated trap configuration into a crossed \ac{ODT}, also generated at \SI{1064}{nm}, in approximately \SI{300}{ms}. This trap consist of two tightly focused beams\footnote{Both generated from a Mephisto MOPA $18$ NE}, a horizontal one with a waist of \SI{25}{\micro m}, and  a vertical one with a waist of \SI{60}{\micro m}. The tighter waist, compared to the reservoir trap, increases the atomic density and improves thermalization. \albi{add reference to the figure of the ODT in the different steps} 

Unlike other fermionic species, highly magnetic lanthanides allow evaporative cooling with a single spin state, thanks to universal dipolar scattering \cite{Lu2012qdd, Aikawa2014rfd}. Here, we perform evaporative cooling with Dy in its lowest hyperfine state, while also sympathetically cooling K. At the end of the evaporative cooling sequence, we routinely reach the deeply degenerate regime, with typical numbers of $N_{\textrm{Dy}}=\SI{1.2e5}{}$, $T_{\textrm{Dy}}/T^{\textrm{Dy}}_F\approx0.13$ and $N_{\textrm{K}}=\SI{2.5e4}{}$, $T_{\textrm{K}}/T^{\textrm{K}}_F\approx0.1$. 

Evaporative cooling of the Dy-K mixture is most effective at low magnetic fields, below \SI{500}{mG}. Above this field, for temperatures $\gtrsim\SI{1}{\micro K}$, strong three-body losses in Dy, also observed in Er \cite{Krstajic2025cot}, prevent efficient evaporative cooling. A more detailed discussion on Dy intraspecies interaction is reported in the next section, while a discussion regarding the limitations of evaporative cooling in particular is reported in Chapter \albi{second to last chapter}.

Over the years, other trapping beams were aligned on the atoms to explore the role of trapping-light wavelength in our experiments (much of Part \hyperref[pt:MakingProbing]{III} is dedicated to this investigation). For the purpose of the mixture preparation, it is important to mention here that after discovering the beneficial role of the $1547$-nm light on the molecular lifetime, two beams, one horizontal and one vertical, generated at this wavelength\footnote{NKT Koheras BOOSTIK E$15$}, were permanently added to the experiment. In particular, the horizontal beam, with waist \SI{90}{\micro m}, has been successfully employed during the evaporative cooling sequence, allowing us to achieve the same degeneracy conditions with a higher number of dysprosium atoms, and in a more robust manner, reducing the day-to-day optimization required. We achieved this by using the $1547$-nm beam at a constant power \albi{check the parameters} throughout the evaporative cooling, while the two tight $1064$-nm beams are exponentially ramped down in power. This additional support against gravity allows us to lower the power of the tight \SI{1064}{nm} beams further, without losing an excessive amount of atoms. In Fig.~\ref{fig:ODTscheme}, we present a recap of the \ac{ODT} scheme, with the two additional \SI{1547}{nm} beams and an \textit{exp beam}, which represents all of the additional beams at different wavelengths tested during my PhD and reported in Chapter \ref{Chpt:MolLoss}.

\section{Feshbach resonances in D\lowercase{y}-K}
\label{Sec:DyKFeshbachOld}

 As previously mentioned, the exceptional complexity of the Dy-K electronic structure precludes a theoretical prediction of the interaction spectrum. Thus the experiment was designed with an exploratory approach, in particular with a pair of large coils in a Helmholtz configuration able to generate magnetic fields up to \SI{400}{G}, considered at the time to be a good range within which to identify a suitable interspecies Feshbach resonance. 
 \begin{figure}[thb]
 	\includegraphics[width=0.8\columnwidth]{gfx/Cpt4_fig/thermScanDyK_200G}
 	\caption{Interspecies thermalization scan around the \SI{200}{G} region. Solid arrows point to the main resonance features. Figure taken from Ref.~\cite{Ravensbergen2020rif}.\label{Fig:ThermScan200G}}
 \end{figure}

The first promising candidates were identified in the magnetic field region around \SI{200}{G}, and reported in Ref.~\cite{Ravensbergen2020rif}. Fig.~\ref{Fig:ThermScan200G} shows the  thermalization measurement between Dy and K in the $200$ G region. Fast thermalization rates are attributed to strong interspecies interactions near Feshbach resonances, indicated by black arrows in the figure. In Sec.~\ref{sec:InterThemScan}, we provide more details on how a thermalization measurement is performed, while in Sec.~\ref{Sec:Compendium}, we extended the magnetic field range of the Feshbach spectroscopy.

The broadest feature observed in the Dy-K mixture is the resonance around \SI{217}{G}. At this Feshbach resonance, strong interactions were demonstrated by observing the expansion of the mixture in \ac{TOF}, see Fig.~\ref{Fig:Hydro200G}. In the weakly interacting regime, i.e.~far from the resonance center, the expansion of the two clouds is ballistic, with the lighter potassium cloud expanding faster than the dysprosium one. On top of the resonance instead, the expansion of the two clouds is similar, as the large number of collisions between the two species locks the lighter potassium inside the heavier dysprosium.This behavior is a hallmark of collisional hydrodynamics and evidence of strong interspecies interactions. We performed a similar experiment when studying the interaction spectrum of the mixture in the low magnetic field region, in particular around a Feshbach resonance we identified at \SI{7}{G}. In Sec.~\ref{sec:HydExp}, we present these results and describe how to interpret them in more detail.

\begin{figure}[thb]
	\includegraphics[width=0.75\columnwidth]{gfx/Cpt4_fig/hydroexpansion200G}
	\caption{Ballistic (upper) and hydrodynamic (lower) expansion of the mixture in the weakly, and strongly interacting regime, respectively. Figure taken from Ref.~\cite{Ravensbergen2020rif}.\label{Fig:Hydro200G}}
\end{figure}

While a conservative estimate of the parameters of the $217$ G resonance predicts a rather large universal regime, suitable for studying strongly correlated many-body phenomena, and lifetimes measured in the vicinity of the resonance are sufficiently long, operating at this magnetic field proved to be challenging over the years and ultimately unsuccessful for multiple reasons. In the first place, the \SI{217}{G} resonance is not well isolated, and numerous narrow features are nested within the broader resonance (see Sec.~\ref{Sec:RF217}) which prevent accurate tuning of the scattering length and cause additional losses. Moreover, the ramp from the preparation field, at \SI{250}{mG}, to the target region proved particularly detrimental, inducing strong heating in the mixture,  because of many interspecies resonances crossed during the ramp. In order to partially reduce these unwanted effects, we developed a method  in which the spatial overlap between the two atomic clouds is reduced using a magnetic field gradient, see Sec.~\ref{subsec:prep} and \ref{SubSec:PrepMixture} for more details.

Despite the valiant efforts of many PhD students, evaporative cooling at a magnetic field higher than \SI{500}{mG} was never achieved. In order to better understand this (apparently fundamental) limitation, the interaction spectrum of ultracold dysprosium in the lowest hyperfine state was studied in a magnetic field range up to \SI{1}{G}, and reported in Ref.~\cite{Soave2022lff}. In Fig.~\ref{Fig:Dy1GFeshbachScan}, the results of the loss spectroscopy measurement are presented. In the $1$ G range under investigation, more than $40$ loss features were identified. We attribute the main limitation to effective evaporative cooling above \SI{500}{mG} to this dense interaction spectrum, particularly owing to the thermal broadening of the resonances, also observed in Er \cite{Krstajic2025cot}. This limitation, not fully understood for a long time, is currently under active investigation, as it will be briefly discussed it in Chapter \ref{Chpt:Outlook}.

To mitigate this limitation, we explored different magnetic field regions. Thanks to improvements in magnetic field control and stability, in particular by purchasing a better power supply\footnote{CAENels FAST-Bi-$1$K$5$} and by using a home-built digital \ac{PID} controller, see Chapter \albi{Appendix A} for more details on both, we identified other promising Feshbach resonances, in particular in the region below \SI{10}{G}. These results are the subject of the following three chapters. First, in Chapter \ref{Chpt:Lowfieldpaper}, we discuss the identification of these low-magnetic-field resonances and their characterization, then, in Chapter \ref{Chpt:DipoleandHydro}, we thoroughly characterize interactions near the \SI{7}{G} resonance by measuring locked oscillations in the hydrodynamic regime. Finally, in Chapter \ref{Chpt:AddDyKRes}, we summarize all additional measurement on the Dy-K interaction spectrum performed over the duration of my PhD. Together, these studies establish the $7$-G resonance as the primary platform for studying the strongly interacting Dy-K mixture.
