While ultracold atom laboratories are in general very complex compared to other table-top experiments, the Dy-K machine is a challenging set up even for the standards of the community. Our is the first experiment to study a quantum mixture which combine an alkali atomic species with a lanthanide one. 

A standard ultracold experiment working with an alkali atom in the bulk, typically uses one or two lasers resonant with the D$2$ and D$1$ lines for cooling, in the \ac{MOT} and gray molasses stages, and one or two high power infrared beams for trapping and evaporative cooling. Adding a second species to such a set up requires additional resonant laser sources for the different transitions of each atomic species. Moreover, lanthanides atoms like dysprosium, erbium, europium \albi{cite} present a richer electronic structure, which can be used to produce colder sub-\unit{\micro\K} atomic samples before evaporative cooling, as is the case in the Dy-K experiment. \albi{this part to be reduced a bit}

 \section{Experimental sequence at a glance}
 \label{Sec:ExpSeq}
 In this section we give an overview of the sequence routinely used in our experiment to produce a doubly degenerate mixture of dysprosium and potassium. This procedure represents the cumulative effort of past and current members of the group \cite{Ravensbergen2018poa,Ravensbergen2020PhD, Tzanova2020PhD, Kreyer2017Master, Soave2022PhD}, and it is presented here in its most current form.
 
In our experiment we use one of the two fermionic isotopes of dysprosium, $^{161}$Dy, combined with the only fermionic isotope of potassium, $^{40}$K. We employ an \textit{all-optical} approach to cool the mixture to degeneracy. Owing to the different magnetic moments of the two atomic species, the magnetic field gradients applied during the \ac{MOT} differ significantly between the two species, which necessitates sequential loading of the two species. Since the magnetic gradient used for potassium is about $5$ times stronger than the one needed for dysprosium, it is convenient to first prepare the K \ac{MOT} first, followed by the Dy \ac{MOT}, in order to avoid perturbing significantly in the Dy cloud. 

Potassium is captured by the $3$D \ac{MOT} from its atomic source, a $2$D \ac{MOT} prepared in a separate glass cell, loaded with an enriched sample of $^{40}$K\,\footnote{The natural abundance of $^{40}$K is about $0.01\%$, so an enriched sample is necessary to work with the fermionic isotope.}. Both $3$D and $2$D \ac{MOT}s operate on the potassium D$2$ line at \SI{767}{nm} (linewidth $\Gamma_{D2}/2\pi\sim\SI{6}{MHz}$), and all the laser beams are generated by the same source\footnote{Toptica TA Pro}. After the \ac{MOT}, potassium is further cooled via a gray molasses stage on the D$1$ line (wavelength \SI{770}{nm} with linewidth $\Gamma_{D1}/2\pi\sim\SI{6}{MHz}$) \albi{missing laser source of the D1}, achieving temperatures of around \SI{30}{\micro K}, with an almost ($\sim80\%$) spin polarised sample in the lowest hyperfine state $\ket{{F}, {m_F}}=\ket{9/2,-9/2}$, with the residual population in the second lowest state  $\ket{9/2,-7/2}$. Simultaneously, to maximize the number of captured atoms, the K cloud is transferred into a large-volume crossed \ac{ODT}, which operates  in the near infrared at a wavelength of \SI{1064}{nm}\,\footnote{Azurlight ALS-IR-$1064$-$50$-I-CC-SF}. \albi{add some ref on the general preparation of K in other systems, see also pra cornee}

Once potassium is trapped in the reservoir \ac{ODT}, the preparation of the Dy cloud can start. The dysprosium atomic source is a high-temperature effusion oven, operating at around $1000\,^\circ$C. The atomic beam is then decelerated by a Zeeman slower that operates on the broad transition at \SI{421}{nm} ($\Gamma_{421}/2\pi\sim\SI{32}{MHz}$). The slowed atomic beam is captured in a $3$D \ac{MOT} generated at the narrow intercombination transition at \SI{626}nm (linewidth $\Gamma_{626}/2\pi\sim\SI{135}{kHz}$). Thanks to the narrowness of transition used in the \ac{MOT}, the cold atomic sample obtained is naturally spin-polarized in the lowest hyperfine state \cite{Dreon2017oca}, that is the stretched state $\ket{{F}, {m_F}}=\ket{21/2, -21/2}$. At the end of the compressed \ac{MOT} stage, the temperature of the atomic cloud is approximately \SI{8}{\micro K}. After the compressed \ac{MOT}, the Dy cloud is also transferred into the same reservoir \ac{ODT} as K. \albi{Dy lights sources missing}

Before the evaporative cooling stage, which is necessary to reach the degenerate regime, Dy undergoes an additional resonant cooling stage\footnote{This additional stage was implemented in the experiment before I joined the team, but reported for the first time in Ref.~\cite{Ye2022ool}, which is part of this thesis as Chapter \ref{Chpt:Lowfieldpaper}.}, performed near the narrow transition at \SI{741}{nm}, with linewidth $\Gamma_{741}/2\pi\sim\,\SI{1.7}{kHz}$ \cite{Lu2011soa}. We perform this cooling stage after transferring the atomic clouds into an elongated \ac{ODT}. Thanks to this cooling stage, we achieve temperatures of around \SI{1}{\micro K} with the Dy cloud. More details on the narrow-line cooling sequence are reported in Sec. \ref{sec:appB}. It is worth noting here that, while as reported in \cite{Ye2022ool} no effect of the \SI{741}{nm} light is observed on the potassium cloud, the rapid changes in the trap configuration strongly affect the potassium cloud, which loses a significant number of atoms. This does not strictly affect the efficiency of evaporative cooling, and has not been a limiting factor in most of the experiments presented in this thesis, but limits the tunability of the potassium atom number. This limitation and potential solutions are discussed in Chapter \albi{one of the concluding chapters}.

At the end of the \SI{741}{nm} cooling stage, the atomic mixture is transferred from the elongated trap configuration into a crossed \ac{ODT}, also generated at \SI{1064}{nm}, in approximately \SI{300}{ms}. This trap consist of two tightly focused beams\footnote{Both generated from the Mephisto MOPA $18$ NE}, a horizontal one with a waist of \SI{25}{\micro m}, and  a vertical one with a waist of \SI{60}{\micro m}. The tighter waist, compared to the reservoir trap, increases the atomic density and improves thermalization. \albi{add reference to the figure of the ODT in the different steps}\albi{also trap freq for the start of evaporation}

Unlike other fermionic species, highly magnetic lanthanides allow evaporative cooling with a single spin state, thanks to universal dipolar scattering \cite{Lu2012qdd, Aikawa2014rfd}. Here, we perform evaporative cooling with Dy in its lowest hyperfine state, while also sympathetically cooling K. At the end of the evaporative cooling sequence, we routinely reach the deeply degenerate regime, with typical numbers of $N_{\textrm{Dy}}=\SI{1.2e5}{}$, $T_{\textrm{Dy}}/T^{\textrm{Dy}}_F\approx0.13$ and $N_{\textrm{K}}=\SI{2.5e4}{}$, $T_{\textrm{K}}/T^{\textrm{K}}_F\approx0.1$. 

Evaporative cooling in the Dy-K mixture has been found to be most effective for low magnetic field, below \SI{500}{mG}. Above this field, for temperatures $\gtrsim\SI{1}{\micro K}$, strong three-body losses in Dy, also observed in Er \cite{Krstajic2025cot}, prevent efficient cooling. A more detailed discussion on Dy intraspecies interaction is reported in the next section, while a discussion regarding the limitations of evaporative cooling in specific is reported in Chapter \albi{second to last chapter}.

During the years, other trapping beams were aligned on the atoms to explore the role of trapping-light wavelength in our experiments (a good portion of Part \hyperref[pt:MakingProbing]{III} is dedicated to this investigation). In particular, the horizontal beam, with waist \SI{90}{\micro m}, has been successfully employed during the evaporative cooling sequence, allowing us to reach the same degeneracy conditions, with an higher number of dysprosium atoms, and in a more robust way, i.e. with less optimization required in the day-by-day running of the experiment. \albi{missing sentence on how we use the 1547, it is not ramped during the evap!} In Fig.~\ref{fig:ODTscheme}, we present a recap of the \ac{ODT} scheme, with the two additional $1547$ beam and an \textit{exp beam}, which represents all of the additional beams at different wavelengths tested during my PhD and reported in Chapter \ref{Chpt:MolLoss}.

\section{Feshbach resonances in D\lowercase{y}-K}
\label{Sec:DyKFeshbachOld}

 As previously mentioned, the sheer complexity of the Dy-K electronic structure prevents any theoretical prediction of the interaction spectrum. Thus the experiment was designed with an explorative approach in mind, in particular with a pair of large coils in Helmholtz configuration able to generate magnetic fields up to \SI{400}{G}, considered at the time to be a good range to find a suitable interspecies Feshbach resonance. 
 
 %After achieving degeneracy, the goal was then to explore the interspecies interaction spectrum, in order to find a suitable Feshbach resonance, where to access the strongly interacting regime. The experimental set-up was planned accordingly with a pair of large coils in Helmholtz configuration, allowing to generate magnetic fields up to \SI{400}{G}.
 \begin{figure}[thb]
 	\includegraphics[width=0.8\columnwidth]{gfx/Cpt4_fig/thermScanDyK_200G}
 	\caption{Interspecies thermalization scan around the \SI{200}{G} region. Solid arrows point to the main resonance features. Figure taken from Ref.~\cite{Ravensbergen2020rif}.\label{Fig:ThermScan200G}}
 \end{figure}

The first interesting candidates were individuated in the region around \SI{200}{G}, and reported in Ref.~\cite{Ravensbergen2020rif}. Fig.~\ref{Fig:ThermScan200G} shows the  thermalization measurement between Dy and K in the \SI{200}{G} region. Fast thermalization rates are attributed to strong interactions from Feshbach resonances, and the center of such resonance is located in the proximity of maxima of thermalization rates, indicated with black arrows in the figure.

The broadest feature observed in the Dy-K mixture is the resonance around \SI{217}{G}. At this Feshbach resonance, strong interaction were demonstrated by observing the expansion of the mixture in \ac{TOF}, see Fig.~\ref{Fig:Hydro200G}. In the weakly interacting regime , i.e. far from the resonance center, the expansion of the two clouds is ballistic, with the lighter potassium cloud expanding faster than the dysprosium one. On top of the resonance instead, the expansion of the two clouds is similar, as the large number of collision between the two species locks the lighter potassium inside the heavier dysprosium. This peculiar behavior can be interpreted as the sign of collisional hydrodynamics and the proof of strong interactions in the mixture. We performed a similar experiment when studying the interaction spectrum of the mixture in the low magnetic field region, in particular around a Feshbach resonance we individuated at \SI{7}{G}. In Sec.\ref{sec:HydExp}, we present this results and describe how to interpret them in more detail.

\begin{figure}[thb]
	\includegraphics[width=0.75\columnwidth]{gfx/Cpt4_fig/hydroexpansion200G}
	\caption{Ballistic (upper) and hydrodynamic (lower) expansion of the mixture in the weakly, and strongly interacting regime, respectively. Figure taken from Ref.~\cite{Ravensbergen2020rif}.\label{Fig:Hydro200G}}
\end{figure}

While a conservative guess on the parameter of the \SI{217}{G} resonance predicts a rather large universal regime, suitable to study many-body phenomena, and lifetimes measured in the proximity of the resonance are sufficiently long, operating at this magnetic field proved to be challenging over the years and ultimately unsuccessful, for multiple reasons. In the first place, the \SI{217}{G} resonance is not well isolated, and presents numerous narrow features nested in the broader one, which prevent accurate tuning of the scattering length and cause additional losses. Moreover, the ramp from the preparation field, at \SI{250}{mG}, to the target region proved to be particularly detrimental, causing strong heating in the mixture,  because of many inter-species resonance crossed during the ramp. In order to partially reduce this unwanted effect, we developed a method where the overlap between the two atomic clouds is reduced using a magnetic field gradient, see Sec.~\ref{subsec:prep} and \ref{SubSec:PrepMixture} for more details.

The ultracold atomic sample, being fermionic and spin polarized, is immune from two-body losses and contact interactions. Minima of atom number are caused by higher partial-waves Feshbach (mostly p-wave) resonances. Figure taken from Ref.~\cite{Soave2022lff}. \label{Fig:Dy1GFeshbachScan}}
\end{figure}


Despite the valiant efforts of many PhD students, evaporative cooling at a magnetic field higher than \SI{500}{mG} was never achieved. In order to better understand this (apparently fundamental) limitation, the interaction spectrum of dysprosium in the lowest hyperfine state was studied in a range of \SI{1}G, and reported in Ref.~\cite{Soave2022lff}. In Fig.~\ref{Fig:Dy1GFeshbachScan}, the result of the loss-spectroscopy measurement is presented. In the \SI{1}G range under investigation, more than $40$ loss feature were individuated. We consider this dense interaction spectrum the main limitation to the realization of effective evaporative cooling at magnetic fields above \SI{500}{mG}, especially due to the thermal broadening of many of the resonances \cite{Krstajic2025cot}. This limitation, not fully understood for a long time, is currently under investigation, and I will briefly discuss it in \albi{one of the final chapters}.


In order to mitigate this limitation, we explored different regions of the magnetic field. Thanks to some improvement to the magnetic field control and resolution, in particular thanks a better power supply\footnote{CAENels FAST-Bi-$1$K$5$} and a home-built digital \ac{PID} controller, see Chapter \albi{Appendix A}, we managed to individuate other promising Feshbach resonances, in the region below \SI{10}{G},


 which are the subject of the next two chapters.
