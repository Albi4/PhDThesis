\chapter{Additional measurements on the D\lowercase{y}-K Feshbach spectrum}
\label{Chpt:AddDyKRes}

This chapter is dedicated to the study of Feshbach resonances outside the low-field region that has been the primary focus of our recent efforts. This work has been done in parallel with the studies reported in Chapter \ref{Chpt:Lowfieldpaper} and \ref{Chpt:MolTrap}, and thus with the crucial contributions of Zhu-Xiong Ye and Elisa Soave. In Section \ref{Sec:Compendium}, we explore the interaction spectrum over a large range of magnetic fields and, in Section \ref{Sec:RF217}, we present an attempt to characterize the broad resonance at \SI{217}{G}.



\section{Compendium of  D\lowercase{y}-K Feshbach resonance}
\label{Sec:Compendium}

All of the main results presented in this thesis are obtained working around the interspecies Feshbach resonance close to \SI{7}{G}. While this resonance proved to be a very fruitful tool for studying interspecies interactions and Feshbach molecules, we also invested some time to re-explore the interaction spectrum in the mixture at different magnetic fields, with the objective, and hope, to find other and possibly broader candidates. A similar study was already performed by  previous member of the team, when looking for a strong and broad resonance, which resulted in the identification of the resonance at \SI{217}{G} reported in Ref.~\cite{Ravensbergen2020rif}. However in the years following that study, considerable effort was made to improve the control and stability of the magnetic field in the experiment\,\footnote{See also Appendix \ref{app:MagneticField} for a presentation of some of these improvements.}, which motivated us to re-explore the entire magnetic field range accessible with our experimental setup.

The approach we employed to identify interspecies resonances is the same as the one presented in Sec.~\ref{sec:InterThemScan}. After evaporative cooling of the mixture, always performed at low magnetic fields, we parametrically heat the potassium atomic cloud and then ramp the magnetic field to a target value, at which the two clouds are allowed to thermalize for a fixed amount of time. Strong interspecies interaction corresponds to peaks in the thermalization rate, which we associate with a Feshbach resonance. The magnetic field resolution is of \SI{20}{mG} for fields below \SI{70}{G}, and of \SI{200}{mG} above that. This choice reflects the fact that below \SI{70}{G}, we were looking for \textit{any} resonance, while above that we were focusing only on identifying \textit{broad} resonances, which would justify the additional troubles of working at high magnetic fields. The coarse scan at higher magnetic fields probably missed multiple narrower features.

The results of this Feshbach spectroscopy are reported in Figures \ref{fig:3_19G_Scan} through \ref{fig:260_360G_Scan}. We identified $42$ interspecies Feshbach resonances investigating magnetic fields up to \SI{360}G, which is close to the upper limit of our coils. Ultimately, no suitable broader resonance was found. No other feature broader than the one at \SI{217}{G} was observed, except for a possible candidate around \SI{340}{G} which appeared to have a somewhat comparable strength. However, we did not characterize this resonance any further as we deemed it to be at too high magnetic field, and  both the large number of resonances that would need to be crossed to reach it and the high magnetic field would not allow us to prepare and control efficiently the quantum mixture. 

All  other resonances observed are of comparable width to, or narrower than, the \SI{7}{G} resonance, but without the advantage of being at low magnetic field. Table \ref{tab:AllFeshbach} summarizes our findings. In Fig.~\ref{fig:3_19G_Scan}, we report the results of the Feshbach spectroscopy up to \SI{19}G, close to \SI3G and \SI7G, we can recognize the resonance discussed in Chapter \ref{Chpt:Lowfieldpaper}, while close to \SI{13}G an additional narrow feature is observed. In Fig.~\ref{fig:19_29G_Scan}, the next magnetic field range is shown, up to \SI{29}G. Here we identify only a single resonance, while in the range from \SI{29}G to \SI{47}G, see Fig.~\ref{fig:29_47G_Scan}, we observe two additional resonances. In Fig.~\ref{fig:47_66G_Scan}, we report the spectroscopy up to \SI{66}G. This region features a particularly dense spectrum, with $11$ resonances observed in a \SI{10}G interval. Some of this resonances, with particular attention to the resonance at \SI{52.83}G, were already reported in Ref.~\cite{Soave2022PhD}, while the resonance at \SI{51.242}G was characterized by measuring the binding energy of the underlying molecular state in Sec.~\ref{sec:BEM}. The large number of resonances in a relatively narrow window of magnetic field discouraged us from pursuing further studies of these resonances. 

Starting from the measurements reported in Fig.~\ref{fig:70_120G_Scan}, we reduced the resolution of the  magnetic field spectroscopy, using now a magnetic field interval of \SI{200}{mG} instead of \SI{20}{mG}, reducing our ability to distinguish narrow resonances. The Feshbach spectroscopy, from \SI{67}{G} up to \SI{180}{G}, presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:70_120G_Scan} and Fig.~\ref{fig:120_180G_Scan}, shows a remarkably low number of resonances, by the standard of the Dy-K mixture. In this \SI{110}{G} interval, we identify only $5$ narrow thermalization features, with a particularly large unperturbed region between \SI{75}G and \SI{140}{G}. In Fig.~\ref{fig:180_260G_Scan}, we report the scan up to \SI{260}{G}, which is the interval that was studied in Ref.~\cite{Ravensbergen2020rif}. At \SI{217}G, the broadest interspecies resonance is clearly visible, together with the other major features reported in the supplementary material of Ref.~\cite{Ravensbergen2020rif}. In the present scan we identify $5$ additional resonances in this range. We present the last magnetic field interval studied in Fig.~\ref{fig:260_360G_Scan}, where the thermalization scan is extended up to \SI{360}G. In this region of the magnetic field we identify multiple resonances with different widths, with multiple narrow resonances and a couple of broader ones. In particular close to \SI{340}G we identify the aforementioned candidate for the second broadest resonance.

\section{Binding energy measurements at the $217$\,G Resonance}
\label{Sec:RF217}

Since the resonance located near \SI{217}{G} is the broadest and strongest resonance identified in the Dy-K mixture, we attempted to characterize it with a  similar approach to the one employed for the low-magnetic-field resonances, i.e. by measuring the binding energy of the molecular state associated with the Feshbach resonance. The experimental approach used closely resembles the one presented in Sec.~\ref{sec:BEM}, albeit at higher magnetic fields. Our motivation here was to apply the techniques developed in Chapter \ref{Chpt:Lowfieldpaper} to a broader resonance and, once the resonance was thoroughly  characterized, use the resonance as a gateway to achieving superfluidity.

\begin{figure}[thb]
	\includegraphics[trim=0cm 0 0cm 0, clip, width=1\columnwidth]{gfx/Cpt6_fig/Binding_energy_217G.pdf}
	\caption{Binding energy of Feshbach molecules around the magnetic field region of the $217$ G resonance. The vertical dashed line indicates the pole of the broad Feshbach resonance, determined in Ref.~\cite{Ravensbergen2020rif}. The points are the results of the magnetic field modulation spectroscopy. The green dashed line is the estimated behavior of the bound state according to Ref.~\cite{Ravensbergen2020rif}. The red and yellow lines are fit results of Eq.~\eqref{equ:bindingEnergyCurve} on subsets of the data. \label{fig:RF_spectra_217G}}
\end{figure}

The results of the magnetic field modulation are reported in Fig.~\ref{fig:RF_spectra_217G}. The interpretation of the results of this measurement is not straightforward. In two separate regions of magnetic field, indicated by the shaded areas in the figure,  the wiggle spectroscopy did not return any meaningful loss feature to associate  with the  molecular spectrum. We attribute this to the presence of narrow Feshbach resonances located at these fields, that significantly complicates  the molecular spectrum underlying the resonances. The presence of additional resonances, not observed in the thermalization spectroscopy, is further corroborated by the behavior of the measured binding energy. In the vicinity of the shaded areas the binding energy values measured deviate from the behavior expected near a broad Feshbach resonance. This discrepancy can be interpreted in terms of avoided crossings between the molecular states underlying the different resonances. Further detuned from resonance, on the lower magnetic field side of the broader feature, we detected multiple loss feature in the results of the wiggle spectroscopy for the same magnetic fields. This strengths the hypothesis of multiple bound states, however the assignment of the different data points to the various resonance is not feasible without additional knowledge on the bound states.

As a guide for the eye, we plotted different possible molecular states in Fig.~\ref{fig:RF_spectra_217G}. The green dashed line is the binding energy, that would be associated to an isolated broad Feshbach resonance, calculated according to Eq.~\eqref{equ:bindingEnergyCurve} using the parameters (differential magnetic moment,  R$^*$ and $A$) estimated in Ref.~\cite{Ravensbergen2020rif}. The red solid line instead is a fit, using the same free parameters as those used in Sec.~\ref{sec:BEM}, of Eq.~\eqref{equ:bindingEnergyCurve} to the data points at magnetic fields $> \SI{215.9}{G}$, i.e.~for the data obtained at magnetic fields above the larger of the two overlapping narrow resonances. The solid orange line is fit for magnetic fields $>\SI{216.5}{G}$, which is for fields above the right most resonance. The gray dashed lines are extrapolations of these fits and serve to suggest the possible behavior of the  bound states but hold no quantitative meaning, as Eq.~\ref{equ:bindingEnergyCurve} is only valid for well isolated resonances. 


In conclusion, we partially characterized the \SI{217}{G} resonance, by measuring the molecular bound state energy. However, this measurement showed that the broad resonance is not well isolated and the presence of narrow resonances prevented a clean characterization. Additionally, these resonances also complicate the precise tuning of interactions and the prospect of reaching the universal regime at the broad resonance. Attempts to perform magneto association of Feshbach molecules at the broad resonance, with a similar approach to what is presented in Chapter \ref{Chpt:MolTrap}, were unsuccessful, as the procedure to follow adiabatically the bound state  were disrupted by the narrow resonances.

\section{Summary}

In this chapter, we have presented two sets of measurements in which we attempted to identify and characterize possible alternatives to the \SI{7}{G} resonance. With a thermalization scan we identify a rich interspecies Feshbach spectrum. We demonstrated that the interaction spectrum of the Dy-K mixture, is extremely rich and in some portions of the magnetic field, the resonance density can be relative high. Narrow resonances with widths of few hundreds of mG appear to be the most common possibility, while larger resonances are more rare.  

We have characterized the molecular spectrum of the \SI{217}{G} resonance, identifying the presence of two narrow features in the vicinity of the pole of the broad resonance. These findings motivated us to stick with the \SI{7}{G} resonance as the most suitable platform for studying the Dy-K mixture in the strongly interacting regime. In the next part of this thesis, we will present our efforts to study and characterize DyK Feshbach molecules associated across the \SI{7}{G} resonance.
