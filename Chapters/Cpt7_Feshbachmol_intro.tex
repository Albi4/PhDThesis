\chapter{Feshbach Molecules as a Doorway to the BEC-BCS Crossover}
\label{Chpt:FeshmolIntro}



While the previous part of this thesis was dedicated towards the individuation and characterization of a suitable interspecies Feshbach resonance between dysprosium and potassium, here we turn our focus to studying the properties of the weakly bound Feshbach DyK molecules associated at the \SI{7}{G} resonance.




Feshbach molecules are of crucial relevance in the context of the \ac{BEC}-\ac{BCS} crossover. In a two-component Fermi gas, the positive-scattering-length side of a Feshbach resonance supports weakly bound dimers composed of one atom from each component. These dimers are composite bosons and, if cooled to sufficiently low temperature, can condense into a \ac{mBEC}. This molecular condensate represents the \ac{BEC} limit of the crossover. On the opposite side of the resonance, where no two-body bound state exists in vacuum, pairing can still occur as a many-body effect, leading to extended Cooper pairs and to the \ac{BCS} limit. The weakly bound Feshbach molecule can thus be viewed as the few-body precursor of the paired many-body state.


Feshbach molecules are therefore not only molecular objects, but also direct manifestations of the resonant interaction itself. As already 



 Their binding energy, magnetic moment, lifetime, and optical response provide information about the character of the underlying Feshbach resonance. This is especially important in systems where coupled-channel calculations are difficult or unavailable. In alkali mixtures, the molecular potentials are often sufficiently well known to guide the search for resonances and to interpret the observed spectra. In contrast, mixtures involving lanthanide atoms, such as our Dy-K system, display a much denser interaction spectrum due to the anisotropic electronic structure and large magnetic moment of dysprosium. For this reason, the spectroscopy of weakly bound DyK molecules becomes an essential diagnostic tool: by measuring the molecular binding energy as a function of magnetic field, one can reconstruct the near-threshold molecular branch and characterize the resonance beyond the information contained in atom-loss spectroscopy alone.



In homonuclear spin mixtures of $^6$Li and $^{40}$K, this connection enabled the first experimental studies of molecular condensates and superfluidity across the \ac{BEC}-\ac{BCS} crossover \cite{Jochim2003bec, Greiner2003eoa, Regal2004oor, Zwierlein2003oob, Zwierlein2004cop}. In heteronuclear mixtures, the situation is richer. If the two fermions have different masses, the resulting molecular condensate is the bosonic limit of a mass-imbalanced Fermi mixture. Such systems are predicted to host pairing phenomena that are not easily accessible in equal-mass gases, including exotic superfluid phases favored by unequal Fermi surfaces.


Feshbach molecules also play a central role in a different, though related, direction of ultracold-molecule research. In many heteronuclear alkali systems, they are used as an intermediate state for coherent transfer to the rovibrational ground state, typically through stimulated Raman adiabatic passage. This route, pioneered in bialkali systems, has led to increasingly advanced molecular quantum gases, including degenerate gases of NaK molecules \cite{Schindewolf2022eom} and \ac{mBEC} of NaCs \cite{Bigagli2024oob} and NaRb \cite{Shi2025bec}. These developments are motivated by dipolar many-body physics, quantum simulation, controlled chemistry, and precision measurements. In the work presented here, however, the weakly bound molecule itself is the main object of interest. 

Producing and stabilizing a gas of such dimers is therefore a natural first step towards the molecular side of the mass-imbalanced \ac{BEC}-\ac{BCS} crossover.
We use DyK Feshbach molecules both as probes of the interspecies resonance and as a possible starting point for future studies of pair condensation in a mass-imbalanced Fermi mixture.

Realizing this program requires more than simply associating atom pairs into molecules. The molecules must be produced efficiently, trapped optically, and remain stable on timescales long enough for further manipulation or cooling. Each of these requirements is nontrivial. Weakly bound Feshbach molecules are fragile objects with small binding energies and large spatial extent. Their properties can depend sensitively on the magnetic field, the optical trapping light, and the surrounding atomic environment. In particular, optical traps can induce losses by coupling the weakly bound molecular state to excited molecular potentials, while collisions with atoms or other molecules can lead to inelastic decay. Understanding these limitations is essential for assessing whether a given resonance is suitable for molecule production, molecular evaporation, and ultimately the realization of a molecular condensate.

The Dy-K system presents both opportunities and challenges in this respect. Compared with alkali mixtures such as Li-K, the presence of dysprosium introduces a richer molecular structure and a denser spectrum of Feshbach resonances. This increases the chance of finding useful resonances, but at the same time makes the theoretical prediction and interpretation of the resonance spectrum more difficult. Related mass-imbalanced Fermi-Fermi systems, such as Li-Cr, share similar long-term goals in the study of heteronuclear pairing, but differ in the degree to which the molecular spectrum can be predicted and controlled. In Dy-K, the experimental characterization of individual resonances and their associated molecular states is therefore a necessary step before many-body experiments can be attempted.

The two publication chapters that follow should be read in this context. The first one reports the production and optical trapping of weakly bound Feshbach molecules of fermionic $^{161}$Dy and $^{40}$K. It establishes the association procedure, measures molecular binding energies, and demonstrates that DyK Feshbach molecules can be trapped in the vicinity of low-field interspecies resonances. The second publication addresses the stability of these molecules and identifies the role of light-induced and collisional losses. Together, these studies provide the first characterization of optically trapped DyK Feshbach molecules and define the main experimental constraints that must be overcome in order to use them as a starting point for future studies of mass-imbalanced pair superfluidity.



The route toward a \ac{mBEC} of DyK molecules, and ultimately toward the study of the BEC-BCS crossover with mass imbalance, requires bringing the molecular sample to quantum degeneracy. Two strategies are in principle available: either starting from a deeply degenerate atomic mixture and performing the association sweep adiabatically, or evaporating the molecules directly after association. In the DyK case, as discussed in Chapter~\ref{Chpt:MolTrap}, the current limiting factor is the molecular lifetime, which constrains both the achievable phase-space density and the time available for further cooling. The results of Chapter~\ref{Chpt:MolLoss} represent significant progress toward overcoming this limitation, by identifying trapping conditions under which light-induced losses are substantially reduced.

Near a Feshbach resonance, the scattering properties of two atoms are governed by the coupling between the open scattering channel and a near-threshold molecular bound state.

When this bound state crosses the atomic threshold, the scattering length becomes resonant; on the molecular side of the resonance, the same bound state appears as a weakly bound dimer, commonly referred to as a Feshbach molecule.
