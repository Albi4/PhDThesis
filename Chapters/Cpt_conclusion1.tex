\chapter{What have we learned?}
\label{Chpt:Summary}

In this chapter we summarize the main results and lessons learned from the Dy-K experiment. In the next chapter, we will instead provide an outlook on future studies achievable with our experiment, and generalize to the study of mass-imbalanced Fermi gases and to unconventional quantum mixtures. %\albi{for now it's not even main results, probably those fits better in the following one}

The first important lesson drawn from the experiment, which is probably not (yet) fully conveyed in the publications from our group, is the difficulty of producing the doubly degenerate mixture. While the evaporative cooling of dysprosium and the simultaneous sympathetic cooling of potassium are reliable and achieve good degeneracy in both species, satisfactory cooling is achieved only at very low magnetic fields, below \SI{500}{mG}. Above this magnetic field, the interaction spectrum of Dy is observed to be strongly temperature-dependent, and severe three-body losses prevent efficient evaporative cooling.  In a first study of the interaction spectrum of $^{161}$Dy \cite{Soave2022lff}, numerous intraspecies resonances were identified for a small magnetic field range, but a complete characterization of these resonances is still lacking. A similar behavior of the intraspecies resonances caused by thermal effects  was identified in a sample of $^{166}$Er \cite{Krstajic2025cot}, however, the density of resonances for the case of bosonic erbium is much lower than for the case of our fermionic dysprosium. These limitations, for a long time not fully explored in order to characterize interactions in the mixture, are currently being studied in our experiment, with the aim of better understanding and mitigating these effects.

A direct consequence of the lack of evaporative cooling at different magnetic fields is the necessity of quickly switching the field, from the preparation region to the vicinity of a Feshbach resonance. Ramping of the magnetic field over potentially several hundred of \unit{G}, limits the achievable degree of degeneracy of the mixture in the strongly interacting regime. During the ramp towards the target field, different inter- and intraspecies resonances are crossed, causing heating in the mixture and reducing the degree of degeneracy. One of the workaround developed in this thesis to address this issue concerns identifying several resonances in the low magnetic field region, closer to the preparation field, thus reducing the number of resonances crossed during magnetic field ramps. More substantially, we developed a procedure in which a magnetic field gradient is used and exploit the different gravitational sag of the two species in order to minimize their overlap during the different magnetic field ramps, as reported in Sec.~\ref{SubSec:PrepMixture}.

The use and reliance on the magnetic field gradient revealed itself to be a double edged sword in our experiments, and while it proved to be an extremely useful tool in several aspects, as we discuss below, its use also introduced certain complications. On one hand it has been an extremely useful tool to control the overlap between the two atomic clouds, not only in reducing it to mitigate unwanted heating effects, but also in optimizing the overlap in order to maximize the efficiency of the magneto-association of DyK Feshbach molecules. Furthermore, the magnetic field gradient proved crucial to the purification scheme we developed to separate the molecular sample from the leftover atoms following the association procedure. This \textit{Stern-Gerlach} purification procedure, see Sec.~\ref{SubSec:SGPurification}, exploits the different magnetic moments and different polarizability between the dimers and the atoms, by engineering a trap configuration that confines only the DyK molecules, trapping neither Dy nor K atoms.

Additionally, with the discovery of light-induced losses the gradient helped to compensate gravity allowing us to keep the light intensity of the trapping beams low, thus increasing the lifetime of the trapped molecular sample. At the same time, however, the lack of a uniform magnetic field complicated working at small detunings from resonance, hindering our ability to study the molecules in the universal open-channel-dominated regime. This effect is caused by the position dependence of the magnetic field and by the finite size of the molecular cloud, which sample different magnetic fields across the span of a few \unit{\micro m}, highlighted at different points throughout this thesis and in particular see Sec.~\ref{Sec:Antitrapping}, \ref{Sec:Lifetime}, and \ref{Sec:Collisions}. This effect is exacerbated by working near a narrow Feshbach resonance, for which variations of a few \unit{mG} can lead to variation of several thousands of $a_0$ and, in the worst-case scenario, an unwanted crossing of the resonance pole, resulting in rapid dissociation of the dimers.
