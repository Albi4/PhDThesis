\chapter{What have we learned?}
\label{Chpt:Summary}

In this chapter we summarize the main lessons learned from the Dy-K experiment and their implications for possible improvements to be made to the experimental setup. In the next chapter, we will provide more physics focused outlook on future studies achievable, starting from the knowledge accumulated in the past years, and generalize to the study of mass-imbalanced Fermi gases and to unconventional quantum mixtures. 

The first important lesson drawn from the experiment, which is probably not (yet) fully conveyed in the publications from our group, is the difficulty of producing the doubly degenerate mixture. While the evaporative cooling of dysprosium and the simultaneous sympathetic cooling of potassium are reliable and achieve good degeneracy in both species, satisfactory cooling is achieved only at very low magnetic fields, below \SI{500}{mG}. Above this magnetic field, the interaction spectrum of Dy is observed to be strongly temperature-dependent, with multiple loss features drastically broadening with increasing temperature, resulting in severe three-body losses that prevent efficient evaporative cooling. In a first study of the interaction spectrum of $^{161}$Dy \cite{Soave2022lff}, numerous intraspecies resonances were identified for a small magnetic field range, but a complete characterization of these resonances is still lacking. A similar behavior of the intraspecies resonances caused by thermal effects  was identified in a sample of $^{166}$Er \cite{Krstajic2025cot}; however, the density of resonances for this case of bosonic erbium is much lower than for the case of our fermionic dysprosium. These limitations, which for a long time were not fully understood, are currently being studied in our experiment, with the aim of better characterizing and mitigating these effects.

A direct consequence of the lack of evaporative cooling at different magnetic fields is the necessity of quickly switching the field, from the preparation region to the vicinity of a Feshbach resonance. This ramping the magnetic field over potentially several hundred of \unit{G}, limits the achievable degree of degeneracy of the mixture in the strongly interacting regime. During the ramp towards the target field, different inter- and intraspecies resonances are crossed, causing heating in the mixture and reducing the degree of degeneracy. One of the workarounds developed in this thesis to address this issue was to identify several resonances in the low magnetic field region, closer to the preparation field, thus reducing the number of resonances crossed during the magnetic field ramps. 

However, while working at the \SI{7}{G} resonance did improve the degeneracy of the mixture, the transfer from the evaporation field still introduces heating. This heating arises from constraints on the achievable magnetic field ramp speed in our experiment. The ramp speed is limited by a combination of factors: the coil size, originally designed for operation at higher fields, and the power supply, designed for much higher currents than those needed to reach \SI{7}{G}. These combined intrinsic limitations cause the mixture to spend a non-negligible time in the strongly interacting regime, which prevents us from exploiting the low magnetic field resonances to their full potential. For a future experiment it would be ideal to select a suitable resonance and design the coils and power supply accordingly. Moreover, to reduce these unwanted heating effects, we developed a procedure in which a magnetic field gradient is used to exploit the different gravitational sag of the two species in order to minimize their overlap during the different magnetic field ramps, as reported in Sec.~\ref{SubSec:PrepMixture}.

The use and reliance on the magnetic field gradient turned out to be a double-edged sword in our experiments, and while it proved to be a powerful resource in several aspects, as we discuss below, its use also introduced certain complications. On one hand, the control of the overlap between the two atomic clouds is beneficial not only to mitigate unwanted heating effects, but also optimizing the overlap helps to maximize the efficiency of the magneto-association of DyK Feshbach molecules. Furthermore, the magnetic field gradient proved crucial to the purification scheme we developed to separate the molecular sample from the leftover atoms following the association procedure. This \textit{Stern-Gerlach} purification procedure, see Sec.~\ref{SubSec:SGPurification}, exploits the different magnetic moments and different polarizabilities between the dimers and the atoms by engineering a trap configuration that confines only the DyK molecules, trapping neither Dy nor K atoms. Additionally, with the discovery of light-induced losses the gradient helped to compensate gravity allowing us to keep the light intensity of the trapping beams low, thus increasing the lifetime of the trapped molecular sample. 

On the other hand, however, the lack of a uniform magnetic field complicated working at small detunings from resonance, hindering our ability to study the molecules in the universal open-channel-dominated regime. This effect is caused by the position dependence of the magnetic field and by both the finite size of the molecular cloud and small oscillations, induced by the complex procedure, of the cloud along the direction of the magnetic field. In this way, the dimers sample different magnetic fields across the span of a few \unit{\micro m}, as highlighted at different points throughout this thesis, in particular in Sec.~\ref{Sec:Antitrapping}, \ref{Sec:Lifetime}, and \ref{Sec:Collisions}. This effect is exacerbated by working near a narrow Feshbach resonance, for which variations of a few \unit{mG} can lead to variations of several thousand of $a_0$ and, in the worst-case scenario, an unwanted crossing of the resonance pole, resulting in rapid dissociation of the dimers.

This limitation is closely intertwined with the problem of light-induced losses. Trapping molecules using an \ac{ODT} alone would require higher light intensities, which, at the wavelengths currently available in our experiment, would lead to rapid dissociation of the dimers. In Chapter~\ref{Chpt:MolLoss}, we identified that longer wavelengths are beneficial and substantially reduce light-induced losses. The best suppression was, however, achieved using a laser at \SI{2002}{nm}, outside the wavelength range for which  the coating of our viewports was originally designed, and preventing us from using optimal trap layouts and fully exploiting the increased lifetime, as reported in Sec.~\ref{Sec:LighInducedLosses}. 

From a more practical point of view, in order to take full advantage of longer wavelengths, a new experimental vacuum chamber would be needed, to fit windows with an appropriate coating. In this regard, a few remarks regarding the vacuum chamber are worth making, in view of possible second-generation experiment. In our setup the experimental main vacuum chamber is a stainless steel octagonal chamber\,\footnote{Kimball physics MCF$800$-G$2$E$5$C$2$A$8$}, which, despite being non-magnetic, generates significant eddy currents, which slows down the magnetic field ramps, and becomes  slightly magnetized over the course of multiple experimental cycles, especially when working repeatedly at high magnetic fields. Additionally, the large size of the chamber does not allow high-resolution imaging, and while the imaging resolution has been sufficient for most of our experiments, as we focused mainly on collective properties, working with small molecular samples already proved challenging. With the goal of exploring exotic many body phases like \ac{FFLO}, which, e.g., could revealed by small modulations of the density only accessible with in-situ imaging \cite{Kinnunen2018tff}, a higher-resolution set up will be necessary. 

A solution to many of these issues would be a second generation experiment designed explicitly to work with a certain resonance, designed to have adequate magnetic field control for the target resonance, and featuring a glass cell vacuum apparatus to avoid eddy currents and magnetization effects completely, in addition to featuring suitable anti-reflection coating for longer infrared wavelengths. While many experiments currently employ an additional ``science'' cell, for the Dy-K case, and other similar mixtures, optical transport of a lanthanide-alkali mixture between cells can be extremely challenging. A solution that is getting more common in the field is to have a  full glass cell in which both preparation and experiments take place. A similar solution is implemented, for example in our group, in the NewlanD experiment of Dr. Emil Kirilov which focuses on the study of bosonic dysprosium \cite{Anich2024cco}. While this experiment also features a \ac{QGM}, for the Dy-K goals such a tool would not be needed, and just a high-resolution imaging with a moderately high numerical aperture would be sufficient. Such a setup would address the main hardware limitations identified in this thesis and position a future experiment to fully exploit the physics accessible with a mass-imbalanced Fermi mixture.
