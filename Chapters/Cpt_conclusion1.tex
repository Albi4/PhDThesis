\chapter{Lessons from an alkali-lanthanide mixture}
\label{Chpt:Summary}




The Dy-K mixture offers a uniquely rich platform for the study of mass-imbalanced fermionic superfluidity, as the results presented in the previous chapters demonstrate. Realizing this platform, however, required pushing the preparation of a degenerate Dy-K system to its limits.In this chapter, we summarize the lessons learned along the way, the strategies we developed to address the challenges encountered, and the extent to which each was successfully overcome. In the next chapter, we will provide more physics-focused outlook on future possible studies, starting from the knowledge accumulated in the past years, and connect to the broader field of unconventional quantum mixtures. 



\textit{Evaporative cooling ---} Evaporative cooling of dysprosium, and the simultaneous sympathetic cooling of potassium, reliably produces a deeply degenerate mixture; however, this is achievable only at very low magnetic fields, below \SI{500}{mG}. Above this field strength, the interaction spectrum of Dy is observed to be strongly temperature-dependent, with multiple loss features drastically broadening with increasing temperature, resulting in severe three-body losses that prevent efficient evaporative cooling. In a first study of the interaction spectrum of $^{161}$Dy \cite{Soave2022lff}, numerous intraspecies resonances were identified over a small magnetic field range, but a complete characterization of these resonances is still lacking. A similar behavior of the intraspecies resonances caused by thermal effects  was identified in a sample of $^{166}$Er \cite{Krstajic2025cot}; however, the density of resonances for this case of bosonic erbium is much lower than for the case of our fermionic dysprosium. Understanding and mitigating these losses is the subject of ongoing work in our experiment, and represents one of the clearest opportunities for improving the degeneracy achievable in future realizations of this and similar mixtures.

\textit{Magnetic field ramps ---} A second limitation, closely related to the first, concerns the transfer of the mixture from the evaporation field to the field of interest for a given experiment. The lack of evaporative cooling at higher magnetic fields results in the necessity of quickly switching the field from the preparation region to the vicinity of a Feshbach resonance, a ramp that can span several hundred \unit{G}. During this ramp, different inter- and intraspecies resonances are crossed, causing heating and reducing the degree of degeneracy of the mixture. We addressed this by identifying and characterizing several resonances in the low magnetic field region, close to the preparation field, in particular the \SI{7}{G} resonance discussed throughout this thesis, which substantially reduces the number of resonances crossed during the ramp and allows us to work with a well-isolated resonance.

While working at the \SI{7}{G} resonance did improve the degeneracy level of the mixture preserved throughout the full experimental cycle, the transfer from the evaporation field still introduces heating. This heating arises from constraints on the achievable magnetic field ramp speed: The coil size, originally designed for operation at higher fields, and the power supply, designed for much higher currents than those needed to reach \SI{7}{G}, cause the mixture to spend a non-negligible time in the strongly interacting regime during the ramp. To partially mitigate this effect, we reduce the density and the spatial overlap of the two clouds before ramping to the target field. This is achieved by modifying the \ac{ODT} configuration along the experimental sequence, in combination with a magnetic field gradient that exploits the different gravitational sag of the two species, as described in Sec.~\ref{SubSec:PrepMixture}. These mitigation strategies come at the price of additional complexity in the experimental sequence, and do not remove the underlying limitation. For a future experiment, it would be ideal to select a target resonance first and design the coils and power supply around it, as discussed at the end of this chapter.



\textit{Magnetic field gradient ---} 
The use and reliance on the magnetic field gradient turned out to be a double-edged sword in our experiments, and while it proved to be a powerful resource in several aspects, as we discuss below, its use also introduced certain complications. On one hand, the control of the overlap between the two atomic clouds is beneficial. Beyond mitigating heating during the field ramps, as discussed above, controlling the overlap also helps maximize the efficiency of the magneto-association of DyK Feshbach molecules. Furthermore, the magnetic field gradient proved crucial to the purification scheme we developed to separate the molecular sample from the leftover atoms following the association procedure. This \textit{Stern-Gerlach} purification procedure, see Sec.~\ref{SubSec:SGPurification}, exploits the different magnetic moments and different polarizabilities between the dimers and the atoms by engineering a trap configuration that confines only the DyK molecules, trapping neither Dy nor K atoms. Additionally, with the discovery of light-induced losses, the gradient helped to compensate gravity allowing us to keep the light intensity of the trapping beams low, thus increasing the lifetime of the trapped molecular sample. 

On the other hand, the absence of a uniform magnetic field complicated working at small detunings from resonance, hindering our ability to study the molecules in the universal open-channel-dominated regime. This effect is caused by the position dependence of the magnetic field and by both the finite size of the molecular cloud and small oscillations, induced by the complex procedure, of the cloud along the direction of the magnetic field. In this way, the dimers sample different magnetic fields across the span of a few \unit{\micro m}, as highlighted at different points throughout this thesis, in particular in Sec.~\ref{Sec:Antitrapping}, \ref{Sec:Lifetime}, and \ref{Sec:Collisions}. This effect is exacerbated by working near a narrow Feshbach resonance, for which variations of a few \unit{mG} can lead to variations of several thousand $a_0$ and, in the worst-case scenario, an unwanted crossing of the resonance pole, resulting in rapid dissociation of the dimers.

\textit{Light-induced losses ---} 
As mentioned above, light-induced losses limit the lifetime of DyK Feshbach molecules in optical traps. Trapping molecules using an \ac{ODT} alone would require higher light intensities, which, at the wavelengths currently available in our experiment, would lead to rapid dissociation of the dimers. In Chapter~\ref{Chpt:MolLoss}, we identified that longer wavelengths are beneficial and substantially reduce light-induced losses, and successfully exploited a laser at \SI{1550}{nm} to observe collisional contributions to the decay process. The best suppression was, however, achieved using a laser at \SI{2002}{nm}, outside the wavelength range for which  the coating of our viewports was originally designed. Identifying this favorable wavelength is itself a significant result of this thesis; fully exploiting it, however, requires a vacuum apparatus designed to support it, as discussed next.

\textit{Vacuum apparatus ---} Fully exploiting these longer wavelengths would require new viewports coatings, and the vacuum chamber raises further practical points, in view of a possible second-generation experiment. In our setup the experimental main vacuum chamber is a stainless steel octagonal chamber\,\footnote{Kimball physics MCF$800$-G$2$E$5$C$2$A$8$}, which, despite being non-magnetic, generates significant eddy currents that slow down the magnetic field ramps, and becomes slightly magnetized over the course of multiple experimental cycles, especially when working repeatedly at high magnetic fields. Additionally, the large size of the chamber does not allow high-resolution imaging, and while the imaging resolution has been sufficient for most of our experiments, as we focused mainly on collective properties, working with small molecular samples already proved challenging. With the goal of exploring exotic many-body phases like \ac{FFLO}, which, e.g., could be revealed by small modulations of the density \cite{Kinnunen2018tff} only accessible with in-situ imaging, a higher-resolution setup will be necessary. 


\textit{Toward a second-generation experiment ---}
The experience accumulated over the years with the Dy-K experiment has brought the preparation of the degenerate mass-imbalanced Fermi mixture close to the limits of the current apparatus. Some of these limits are physical in nature, such as the interaction spectrum of dysprosium, and are the subject of ongoing investigation. Others are technical, and they point to a clear set of design choices for the next step: A second-generation experiment built around the \SI{7}{G} resonance and the physics it enables. Such an apparatus would feature magnetic field coils and power supplies designed for the target field, which allow fast and reproducible ramps. It would also have a glass-cell vacuum system without eddy currents or magnetization effects, and optical access suited to the longer wavelengths found to minimize light-induced losses. While many experiments currently employ an additional ``science" cell, for the Dy-K case, and other similar mixtures, optical transport of a lanthanide-alkali mixture between cells can be extremely challenging. A solution that is getting more common in the field is to have
a full glass cell in which both preparation and experiments take place. Finally, it would have imaging with higher resolution. A similar single-cell approach is already implemented in our group in the NewlanD experiment of Dr.~Emil Kirilov \cite{Anich2024cco,Hoellrigl2026ssp}. While that experiment also features a \ac{QGM}, a high-resolution imaging system with a moderately high numerical aperture would be sufficient for the Dy-K goals. Such a setup would address the main hardware limitations identified in this thesis and position a future experiment to fully exploit the physics accessible with a mass-imbalanced Fermi mixture.
