\chapter{Summary and Outlook}
\label{Chpt:Outlook}


In this thesis, we have presented a thorough characterization of the interaction properties of a mass-imbalanced Fermi-Fermi mixture of a lanthanide and an alkali atomic species. In the first part of the thesis, in the pursuit of a suitable, broad, and well-isolated interspecies Feshbach resonance, we identified more than $40$ resonances over a range of magnetic fields, in a range from $0$ to \SI{360}{G}. Among these, in Chapter~\ref{Chpt:Lowfieldpaper}, we focused on resonances in the low-magnetic-field region below \SI{10}{G}, identifying a particularly promising candidate near \SI{7}{G} to realize and control strong interactions in the Dy-K mixture. In Chapter~\ref{Chpt:DipoleandHydro}, we characterized the interaction close to the $7$-G resonance in detail, investigating the strongly interacting regime by observing the collisional hydrodynamic crossover. 

In the second half of the thesis, we focused on the study of DyK Feshbach molecules. Working close to the $7$-G resonance, we developed a protocol to associate and purify an optically trapped sample of DyK molecules, and we characterized the resonance parameters through independent measurements of the molecular bound state, as reported in Chapter~\ref{Chpt:MolTrap}. While the path toward the \ac{BEC}-\ac{BCS} crossover via molecular condensation remains ongoing, the study of the molecular lifetime in Chapter~\ref{Chpt:MolLoss} proved to be scientifically rich in its own right. We identified and partially mitigated the dominant light-induced loss process limiting the molecular lifetime, and we investigated the role of collisional losses, observing Pauli suppression of two-body processes for very weakly bound dimers. %i.e.~for small detuning from resonance. 
These results set the stage for further studies of the Dy-K mixture aiming at achieving and exploring superfluidity in a mass-imbalanced Fermi gas. A more technical assessment of the lessons learned, in view of a second-generation experiment, has been presented in Chapter~\ref{Chpt:Summary}. To conclude this thesis, I would like to briefly look forward.

The most natural next step is the realization of a molecular Bose-Einstein condensate of DyK dimers. This would represent the bosonic limit of the \ac{BEC}-\ac{BCS} crossover in a mass-imbalanced system, and would open the door to the study of the full crossover from a \ac{mBEC} to the fermionic superfluid regime. The results of Chapter~\ref{Chpt:MolLoss} represent significant progress toward this goal: By identifying trapping conditions where light-induced losses are substantially suppressed, we have extended the molecular lifetime to the point where further evaporation becomes conceivable. Reaching a \ac{mBEC} will likely require a combination of improved trapping conditions exploiting longer-wavelength light sources, as suggested by our results, and a more degenerate starting mixture, which in turn requires addressing the evaporative cooling limitations discussed in Chapter~\ref{Chpt:Summary}. Once a sufficiently degenerate and long-lived molecular sample will be available, the study of the full \ac{BEC}-\ac{BCS} crossover in a mass-imbalanced system become accessible, including the search for the exotic pairing phases, such as the \ac{FFLO} state, which motivated the construction of the experiment in the first place.

Beyond the molecular side of the crossover, the atomic mixture itself offers interesting directions that are worth pursuing. The study of  collective oscillations, and in particular the dipole-mode spectrum presented  in Chapter~\ref{Chpt:DipoleandHydro}, was performed at a fixed degeneracy level, close to the thermal regime. A natural extension would be to map out the temperature dependence of the hydrodynamic crossover, tracking how the onset of collisional hydrodynamics evolves as the mixture is brought closer to degeneracy. This would provide a more complete picture of the strongly interacting regime and could reveal signatures of many-body pairing around the superfluid transition.

On the experimental side, the most pressing challenge remains the mitigation of Dy intraspecies losses at finite magnetic field, which currently prevent evaporative cooling above \SI{500}{mG} and it's one of the main limiting factors to the degeneracy level achievable in the mixture. Our current efforts are focused on better understanding and controlling these losses by exploiting reduced geometries, in particular, by confining the gas in lower-dimensional trapping configurations where the density of states and the collision dynamics are modified. Whether preliminary results from this direction will mature into a concrete mitigation strategy remains to be seen, but I am cautiously optimistic that a path forward exists. A longer-term solution, as discussed in Chapter~\ref{Chpt:Summary}, would be a second-generation experiment designed from the outset around the target resonance and its magnetic field requirements.

The Dy-K experiment has been one of the first to explore the combination of a lanthanide and an alkali atomic species in a quantum degenerate mixture, and for a long time it operated largely without theoretical guidance on the interaction properties of the system. This situation is beginning to change. In the last few years, increasing theoretical interest in exotic alkali-lanthanide mixtures has started to produce quantitative results, such as estimates of van der Waals coefficients for Er and Dy mixed with alkali or alkaline-earth species \cite{Kopczyk2024vdw} or a proposal on more exotic systems combining a transition metal with a lanthanide \cite{Ciamei2026uhs}. Concurrently, new experiments are beginning to explore similar systems: the first observation of Feshbach resonances in Er-Li \cite{Schafer2022fro}, the preparation of doubly degenerate Bose-Fermi \cite{Karpov2026ico} and Bose-Bose \cite{Kalia2026coa} mixtures of Er-Li, early studies of Er-K \cite{Klaus2026}, and the observation of Feshbach resonances in a Bose-Fermi Dy-Li mixture \cite{Xie2025fso}, suggest that this class of mixtures is attracting growing interest from the community. 

A century after the foundations of quantum statistics were laid, ultracold atoms allow us to engineer quantum systems that the pioneers of the field could hardly have imagined. Mass-imbalanced Fermi gases and alkali-lanthanide mixtures are two young and closely connected directions of this effort. The first promises access to unconventional pairing and superfluidity, while the second offers a rich and largely unexplored landscape of interactions and molecules. The Dy-K experiment sits at the crossing of the two. With new experiments and new theoretical work appearing around the world, both directions are becoming increasingly accessible, and the results presented in this thesis provide a solid starting point for each of them.
